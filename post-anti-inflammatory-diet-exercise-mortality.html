New Study: Anti-Inflammatory Diet Plus Exercise Cuts Mortality Risk in Older Adults

A large prospective cohort study published in August 2026 followed more than 6,000 Chinese older adults for seven years and found that eating an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern was associated with a meaningful drop in all-cause mortality, with the benefit amplified further when combined with regular physical activity.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes, according to a 2026 prospective cohort study of 6,468 older adults. Participants who scored highest on an anti-inflammatory diet index had a 15% lower risk of dying from any cause over a seven-year follow-up compared to those with the lowest scores. When a high anti-inflammatory diet was combined with regular exercise or high leisure activity, the protective association was even stronger. The findings add large-scale observational data to a growing body of evidence linking dietary inflammation control and physical activity to longer, healthier lives.

Inflammation is increasingly understood as one of the central mechanisms through which lifestyle shapes how we age. The concept of "inflammaging," a term researchers use to describe the chronic, low-grade inflammation that accumulates as the body gets older, has become one of the most active areas in aging science. What we eat and how much we move are two of the most tractable levers we have for influencing inflammaging, and a major new study has put numbers to just how much they matter together.

The study, published August 19, 2026, in Frontiers in Immunology, is the first to examine the joint effect of an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern and physical activity on all-cause mortality in a Chinese older adult population. Its findings strengthen the case that the combination of eating a less inflammatory diet and staying physically active is not just good for biomarkers on paper, but is measurably associated with living longer.

The dietary inflammatory index (DII) is a scoring tool developed by nutrition researchers to quantify how pro- or anti-inflammatory a person's overall diet is, based on the published effect of specific nutrients and food components on inflammatory markers including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-alpha.

What the Study Found

The research team at Jinzhou Medical University analyzed data from 6,468 adults aged 65 and older enrolled in the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey. Participants were followed for a median of seven years, during which 4,117 deaths occurred, giving the study considerable statistical power to detect real associations rather than noise. Anti-inflammatory diet quality was measured using the anti-inflammatory diet index (AIDI), a validated scoring tool derived from food frequency questionnaire data that rates dietary patterns from most pro-inflammatory to most anti-inflammatory based on their effects on known inflammatory biomarkers.

The headline result was clear: compared with participants in the lowest third of anti-inflammatory diet scores, those in the highest third had a 15% lower risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 0.85; 95% confidence interval 0.77 to 0.94). Put differently, eating a more anti-inflammatory diet was associated with meaningful protection against premature death over a seven-year window. The dose-response relationship also held up on a continuous scale: each one standard deviation increase in the AIDI was linked to an 8% lower mortality risk (Sun et al., Frontiers in Immunology, 2026).

The more striking finding may be what happened when diet and physical activity were combined. Participants who achieved both a high anti-inflammatory diet score and regular exercise, or high anti-inflammatory diet and high leisure activity, showed significantly lower mortality risk than those who met only one criterion or neither. The joint analysis suggests the two lifestyle behaviors do not simply add up in parallel, but may reinforce each other in ways that compound their protective effect. Researchers noted the findings "underscore the value of integrating both dietary and physical activity strategies to promote healthy longevity."

Why Inflammation Is Central to Aging and Mortality Risk

To understand why these findings matter, it helps to understand what inflammaging actually is and why it shortens lives. As the body ages, the immune system gradually shifts into a state of low-level, chronic activation. This is distinct from the acute inflammation that heals a wound or fights an infection, which is short-lived and resolves. Inflammaging is a smoldering background signal that does not resolve, and it is driven by accumulating cellular damage, senescent cells that release inflammatory molecules, changes in the gut microbiome, and immune system aging itself.

A 2022 review in the journal Cells described inflammaging as "one of the seven pillars of ageing" and detailed how it drives multimorbidity and mortality risk. Elevated levels of inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha are consistently associated across large studies with higher risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, cognitive decline, and cancer, four of the leading causes of death in older adults worldwide. Reducing the background level of inflammation through modifiable lifestyle behaviors is therefore not merely a wellness goal, but a survival strategy (Teissier, Boulanger, and Cox, Cells, 2022).

Diet is one of the most powerful modulators of inflammatory tone available to most people, because it provides a constant daily signal to the immune system. Every meal shifts the body toward more or less inflammatory signaling depending on its composition. Foods high in refined carbohydrates, trans fats, and ultra-processed ingredients activate pro-inflammatory pathways, while foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids, polyphenols, fiber, and certain vitamins and minerals push in the opposite direction. The cumulative effect of these daily signals over years and decades is substantial.

How Diet Shapes CRP and Other Inflammatory Markers

The connection between dietary patterns and CRP, the most commonly used clinical marker of systemic inflammation, is well established in the literature. A 2024 meta-analysis published in Medicine pooled data from 13 cross-sectional studies covering 54,813 participants and found that people eating the most pro-inflammatory diets had 25% higher odds of elevated CRP compared with those eating the most anti-inflammatory diets (odds ratio 1.25; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.32). The association was robust across geographic regions including Asia, Europe, and the United States, and held up in sensitivity analyses (Hua et al., Medicine, 2024).

An earlier systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies with nearly 60,000 participants reinforced this picture at an even more granular level. Researchers found that each one-unit increase in the dietary inflammatory index as a continuous variable was associated with a 10% increase in the odds of having elevated CRP (odds ratio 1.10; 95% CI 1.06 to 1.14). This dose-response relationship is particularly meaningful because it implies there is no threshold below which dietary inflammation stops mattering, and no ceiling above which cleaning up your diet stops helping (Mohammadi et al., Food Science and Nutrition, 2023).

What foods drive these differences? The anti-inflammatory diet index used in the 2026 mortality study, like the broader dietary inflammatory index literature, scores favorably on items including vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, fish, and olive oil, and scores unfavorably on refined grains, processed meats, sugary beverages, and fried foods. This pattern maps closely onto what researchers call "dietary inflammatory burden," and it is no coincidence that the Mediterranean diet, which emphasizes most of the high-scoring foods, consistently shows the strongest evidence for reducing CRP and lowering mortality risk.

What Physical Activity Adds

Exercise has its own well-established anti-inflammatory effect, operating through mechanisms distinct from, but complementary to, diet. Acute exercise transiently raises inflammatory markers, including CRP, in the hours immediately after a hard session, a response the body uses to trigger muscle repair and adaptation. But regular, moderate-to-vigorous exercise, over weeks and months, produces the opposite pattern at rest: lower resting CRP, lower IL-6, lower inflammatory tone generally. This is thought to happen partly because exercise reduces visceral fat, a key driver of inflammaging, and partly because skeletal muscle itself produces anti-inflammatory molecules called myokines during contraction.

The amplifying effect of combining diet with exercise, as observed in the 2026 mortality study, fits neatly into the broader understanding of how lifestyle factors compound. A 2025 review published in Frontiers in Immunology described how immunosenescence and inflammaging can be modulated through integrative strategies combining nutrition and lifestyle interventions, specifically noting that physical activity and anti-inflammatory dietary patterns each work on partly overlapping and partly independent pathways, making them additive or even synergistic in their effects on systemic inflammatory markers (Muller and Di Benedetto, Frontiers in Immunology, 2025). The practical implication: doing both matters more than doing either alone.

What an Anti-Inflammatory Dietary Pattern Looks Like in Practice

The anti-inflammatory diet index used in the Chinese longevity study is derived from a validated scoring framework that assigns positive or negative inflammatory scores to individual foods and nutrients. In broad terms, the pattern it rewards looks like this: a high proportion of calories from whole, minimally processed plant foods, including a wide variety of vegetables, fruits, legumes, and whole grains; regular consumption of fatty fish providing omega-3 fatty acids; liberal use of olive oil and nuts; modest amounts of lean poultry; and limited intake of red meat, processed meats, refined flour and sugar, fried foods, and commercially prepared snacks.

It is worth noting that this pattern closely mirrors the Mediterranean and DASH dietary patterns that have the strongest evidence base for longevity and cardiovascular protection. Researchers often point out that no single food is anti-inflammatory on its own; the benefit comes from the overall dietary pattern maintained over months and years. A piece of salmon is not meaningful if it sits alongside a diet otherwise dominated by ultra-processed foods. Consistency and overall composition matter more than any individual food choice.

For older adults specifically, protein adequacy deserves particular attention within an anti-inflammatory eating pattern. Sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle mass, is itself pro-inflammatory and independently associated with higher mortality risk. Eating enough high-quality protein from fish, legumes, eggs, and dairy while following an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern supports muscle maintenance alongside inflammation control. The 2026 study's finding that physical activity and diet compound each other may partly reflect the fact that exercise requires and reinforces adequate protein intake for muscle repair, closing a virtuous loop.

How CRP Tracking Can Support Lifestyle Monitoring

One of the challenges of adopting and sustaining an anti-inflammatory lifestyle is that the benefits are largely invisible in the short term. You cannot feel your CRP dropping, and the long-run mortality benefit documented in studies like this one plays out over years. This is where measuring CRP as a general wellness marker can be useful, not as a diagnostic tool, but as a feedback signal that gives some visibility into whether lifestyle changes are moving your biology in the right direction.

CRP is the downstream output of everything shaping your inflammatory state, from diet and exercise to sleep, stress, and body composition. When dietary changes, more consistent exercise, and better sleep come together, the improvement tends to show up in CRP levels within weeks to a few months. Following trends in CRP over time can help you see whether the changes you are making are working, and can flag a period of elevated inflammation worth discussing with a healthcare provider. As with any wellness biomarker, CRP values are a starting point for a conversation, not a diagnosis, and persistent elevations should always prompt a medical consultation.

The broader lesson from the 2026 mortality study is that the levers we already know about, eating less inflammatory foods and moving more, are not just good for how we feel today. They are measurably associated with living longer. That is a finding worth taking seriously, especially given how tractable both behaviors are. No prescription required, and the dose-response relationship means that every improvement in dietary quality and every increment of additional physical activity counts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the anti-inflammatory diet index (AIDI)?

The anti-inflammatory diet index (AIDI) is a validated scoring tool that rates the overall inflammatory potential of a person's diet based on how different foods and nutrients are known to affect inflammatory biomarkers such as CRP, IL-6, and TNF-alpha. Higher scores indicate a more anti-inflammatory dietary pattern, typically one rich in vegetables, fruits, fish, whole grains, legumes, and olive oil, with limited processed meats, refined grains, and sugary foods.

Does exercise make an anti-inflammatory diet more effective for longevity?

Based on the 2026 prospective cohort study by Sun et al., the combination of a high anti-inflammatory diet score with regular exercise or high leisure activity was associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality than either behavior alone. This suggests the two lifestyle strategies may be complementary, working through partly independent biological pathways to reduce inflammation and extend healthy life.

How does an anti-inflammatory diet affect CRP levels?

Multiple meta-analyses show that eating a more pro-inflammatory diet is associated with measurably higher CRP levels. A 2024 meta-analysis of 13 studies covering 54,813 participants found that the most pro-inflammatory diets were linked to 25% higher odds of elevated CRP compared with the most anti-inflammatory diets. A 2023 meta-analysis found each one-unit increase in the dietary inflammatory index was associated with a 10% increase in the odds of elevated CRP, suggesting a continuous dose-response relationship.

What foods should older adults eat to reduce inflammation?

Research consistently supports a dietary pattern built on vegetables, fruits, legumes, whole grains, fatty fish rich in omega-3 fatty acids, olive oil, and nuts, with limited intake of ultra-processed foods, refined sugars, processed meats, and fried foods. This pattern, which overlaps substantially with the Mediterranean diet, is associated with lower CRP levels and, as the 2026 mortality study showed, with meaningfully lower all-cause mortality over long follow-up periods. For personalized dietary advice, a registered dietitian or healthcare provider is the appropriate resource.
