New Review: Cardiorespiratory Fitness Beats Diet Quality for Longevity, Mayo Clinic Analysis Finds

A November 2026 review in Mayo Clinic Proceedings compared decades of research on diet and exercise and found that physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness have a stronger link to longevity than diet quality, with high fitness largely eliminating excess mortality from a lower-quality diet.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

A comprehensive review published November 11, 2026 in Mayo Clinic Proceedings found that physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness are more strongly associated with longevity than diet quality. Sedentary behavior was linked to far greater excess mortality than poor diet, and high fitness largely offset the mortality risk of eating less healthfully. The authors argue that public health priorities have over-emphasized diet at the expense of exercise, and that both matter but fitness deserves more attention. This distinction is relevant to inflammation: exercise is one of the most reliable tools for lowering CRP, the main blood marker of systemic inflammation.

For decades, nutrition has dominated public health messaging. The advice to eat more vegetables, cut processed foods, and follow a Mediterranean-style diet is backed by real evidence. But a comprehensive new analysis suggests that the emphasis on diet relative to physical activity may be out of proportion with what the science actually shows about how long people live. If you are going to invest effort in one lifestyle change for longevity, the emerging data suggest that moving more may return a larger dividend than eating perfectly.

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) refers to how efficiently your heart and lungs deliver oxygen to working muscles during sustained physical effort. It is typically measured as VO2 max, the maximum rate at which your body can use oxygen, and is widely considered one of the strongest objective predictors of long-term health and mortality.

What the New Review Found

The analysis, published November 11, 2026 in Mayo Clinic Proceedings, was authored by Glenn A. Gaesser of Arizona State University and Robert E. Sallis of Kaiser Permanente. The researchers reviewed meta-analyses and prospective cohort studies that directly compared diet quality, physical activity, and cardiorespiratory fitness as predictors of all-cause mortality. Rather than asking whether each factor matters in isolation, they asked which matters more when you put them side by side, and what happens when one factor is high and the other is low. The findings, taken together, consistently favored physical activity and fitness over diet quality (Gaesser and Sallis, Mayo Clinic Proceedings, 2026).

The authors summarized five key patterns from the evidence. First, a sedentary lifestyle is associated with substantially greater excess mortality risk than poor diet quality. Second, the reduction in all-cause mortality linked to high physical activity or cardiorespiratory fitness exceeds the reduction linked to high adherence to healthy dietary patterns or plant-based eating. Third, improvements in physical activity or fitness are more strongly associated with lower mortality than improvements in diet quality. Fourth, in studies that looked at both factors together, physical activity or fitness consistently showed a stronger association with mortality than diet, and several studies found that high fitness largely erased the excess mortality risk that comes with a lower-quality diet. Fifth, exercise can prevent or offset the adverse cardiometabolic effects of short-term unhealthy dietary choices, while dietary interventions are largely ineffective at offsetting the physiological consequences of physical inactivity.

The authors are careful to frame this as a rebalancing, not a dismissal of diet. They write that the findings do not diminish the importance of a healthy diet but indicate that the disproportionate focus on diet relative to physical activity in current public health priorities is not supported by the comparative evidence. Their recommendation is for greater emphasis on increasing physical activity and improving cardiorespiratory fitness when the primary goals are longevity and reduction of chronic disease mortality. The full review is available in Mayo Clinic Proceedings.

How Large Is the Sedentary Risk?

The comparison between sitting and eating poorly is striking when you look at the numbers. A landmark 2019 meta-analysis in the British Medical Journal analyzed accelerometer-measured physical activity and sedentary time in 36,383 adults followed for a median of 5.8 years. The most sedentary participants had a mortality hazard ratio of 2.63 compared to the least sedentary, meaning their risk of dying during follow-up was 163 percent higher. By contrast, the most physically active participants had a hazard ratio of just 0.27 compared to the least active, representing a 73 percent reduction in mortality risk. Any physical activity at any intensity, including light walking, was associated with meaningful reductions in mortality (Ekelund et al., BMJ, 2019).

Diet quality studies, while genuinely important, rarely show reductions of that magnitude. An anti-inflammatory dietary pattern, for example, was associated with a 15 percent reduction in all-cause mortality in a 2026 prospective cohort study of 6,468 older Chinese adults followed for seven years. That is a clinically meaningful effect, and the combination of an anti-inflammatory diet with regular exercise produced even greater risk reduction. But the 15 percent mortality reduction from dietary adherence is considerably smaller than the 73 percent reduction associated with being among the most physically active (Sun et al., Frontiers in Immunology, 2026).

This gap is not a reason to abandon a healthy diet. Both effects are real, and they stack. But it does help explain why the Gaesser and Sallis review concludes that the public health emphasis has been misaligned, and why fitness may be the more powerful lever to pull if you are deciding where to focus your energy.

The Inflammation Connection

One reason exercise is so powerfully linked to longevity is its effect on systemic inflammation, which is the shared biological mechanism behind most chronic disease. CRP, the standard blood marker of inflammation, is elevated in people with sedentary lifestyles and tends to fall with consistent physical activity. This is not incidental. Inflammation is the common thread running through cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, Alzheimer's disease, and metabolic syndrome. Lowering it through fitness reduces risk across all of these conditions simultaneously, which likely explains much of the mortality advantage that physically fit people enjoy.

The anti-inflammatory effect of exercise operates through several pathways. Aerobic activity triggers the release of myokines, chemical messengers released by contracting muscle that suppress the inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-alpha. Regular training improves insulin sensitivity, which reduces the low-grade inflammation that tracks closely with insulin resistance. It reduces visceral fat, the fat depot that sits around organs and is itself a source of inflammatory signals. And over time, it lowers resting CRP in a dose-dependent way, with more consistent exercise producing more sustained reductions.

A 2024 meta-analysis of combined aerobic and resistance training in people with type 2 diabetes and overweight found significant reductions in CRP, IL-6, and TNF-alpha compared to control conditions. This demonstrates that the anti-inflammatory effects of exercise are measurable and meaningful even in people starting from a state of metabolic stress, the population at highest risk. The benefit was not marginal: across 20 eligible trials covering nearly 1,200 participants, the inflammatory markers moved in the right direction consistently (Al-Mhanna et al., PeerJ, 2024).

Diet also reduces CRP, and an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern is a proven tool for managing systemic inflammation. Foods rich in polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, fiber, and antioxidants all support lower inflammatory tone. The point is not that diet is irrelevant to inflammation, but that exercise may exert a broader and more robust anti-inflammatory effect across a wider range of people. And crucially, the Gaesser and Sallis review found that exercise can offset the inflammatory consequences of dietary slippage, while dietary improvements alone cannot compensate for the inflammatory burden of prolonged inactivity.

Why Diet Still Matters, Even If Exercise Matters More

The Gaesser and Sallis review is not a license to eat poorly if you work out. The authors are explicit that both a healthy diet and regular physical activity remain important public health goals, and that the evidence for diet quality as a longevity factor is real. What the review challenges is the disproportionate emphasis on diet in public health campaigns, nutrition guidelines, and consumer wellness culture, an imbalance they argue is not justified by comparative data.

Diet and exercise interact in ways that make both more valuable together than either is alone. The 2026 Frontiers in Immunology cohort study of older Chinese adults found that people who combined a high anti-inflammatory diet score with regular exercise had significantly lower mortality risk than those who followed just one behavior. This additive effect makes intuitive sense: the two behaviors attack inflammation and metabolic risk through different pathways, and stacking them compounds the benefit.

Diet quality also shapes what exercise can accomplish. A body that is chronically inflamed from excess refined sugars, industrial trans fats, and ultra-processed foods is a body that recovers more slowly from workouts, builds less muscle from resistance training, and adapts less efficiently to aerobic stress. Getting both right creates a feedback loop where each habit reinforces the other. The practical implication of the new review is not to abandon nutrition but to recognize that the gym membership may be doing more for your longevity than the organic grocery haul, and to invest accordingly in time, energy, and consistency.

What "Cardiorespiratory Fitness" Actually Means Day to Day

Cardiorespiratory fitness is not an abstract lab metric. It is a real-world reflection of how much aerobic stress your heart, lungs, and muscles can handle. People with high CRF can walk up stairs without getting winded, sustain a brisk pace over distance, and recover quickly after exertion. People with low CRF tire quickly, have higher resting heart rates, and are more likely to show the metabolic markers, including elevated CRP, that track with chronic disease risk.

The good news is that CRF is highly trainable, and significant improvements are possible at any age. Zone 2 aerobic training, the kind that keeps your heart rate at a conversational intensity for extended periods, is one of the most evidence-backed methods for building it. So is brisk walking, cycling, swimming, and any sustained rhythmic activity. Even moderate increases in physical activity from a low baseline produce measurable CRF improvements and corresponding reductions in inflammatory markers. You do not need to be an athlete to close a meaningful fraction of the mortality gap the research describes.

Resistance training adds its own dimension. Preserving muscle mass through strength training protects against sarcopenia, the age-related muscle loss that drives frailty and metabolic decline, and it independently reduces inflammatory markers. The combination of aerobic and resistance exercise in a consistent weekly routine appears to be more effective than either type alone for lowering CRP and improving overall cardiometabolic health. Most exercise guidelines recommend aiming for both.

Tracking Whether Your Routine Is Working

One practical implication of this research is the value of measuring inflammation directly. CRP is the most widely used clinical proxy for the systemic inflammation that connects fitness, diet, and long-term disease risk. It reflects the net effect of everything happening in your body, from your activity level and diet to your sleep, stress, and body composition, in a single number. Because it is not driven by any single input, it can capture changes that are hard to detect otherwise.

If you are building a consistent exercise habit and wondering whether it is actually lowering your inflammatory load, a CRP test before and after several weeks of training gives you objective evidence. Research consistently shows that CRP falls with sustained aerobic and resistance training in people who start from an elevated baseline. The drop may be modest in any given period, but over months it accumulates into a meaningful change in cardiometabolic risk. Tracking it longitudinally, rather than taking a single snapshot, is the most informative approach.

CRP is a general wellness marker, not a diagnostic tool. Persistent elevations in CRP, particularly high-sensitivity CRP above 3 mg/L, are worth discussing with a healthcare provider, since they can reflect a range of underlying conditions that warrant evaluation. As a data point for general wellness tracking, it is most useful as a trend over time, one more piece of information in the broader picture of how your body is responding to the lifestyle changes you are making.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does exercise matter more than diet for longevity?

According to a November 2026 review in Mayo Clinic Proceedings by Gaesser and Sallis, physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness show a stronger association with all-cause mortality than diet quality in comparative analyses. High fitness largely offsets excess mortality from a lower-quality diet, while dietary improvements alone do not compensate for the mortality risk of physical inactivity. Both matter, but the evidence suggests exercise deserves greater public health emphasis.

How does exercise reduce inflammation?

Aerobic exercise triggers the release of anti-inflammatory myokines from contracting muscle, reduces visceral fat (a source of inflammatory cytokines), improves insulin sensitivity, and lowers resting CRP over time. These effects occur through several pathways simultaneously, which is why consistent physical activity is one of the most reliable tools for reducing systemic inflammation and the chronic disease risk that comes with it.

What is cardiorespiratory fitness and how do I improve it?

Cardiorespiratory fitness, often measured as VO2 max, reflects how efficiently your heart and lungs deliver oxygen during sustained exertion. It is one of the strongest predictors of longevity. It improves with consistent aerobic exercise: brisk walking, cycling, swimming, jogging, and zone 2 training (sustained effort at a conversational intensity) are among the most accessible methods. Even modest improvements from a low baseline significantly reduce mortality risk.

Can I use a CRP test to track whether my exercise habits are working?

Yes. CRP reflects systemic inflammation driven by your combined lifestyle, including activity, diet, sleep, and body composition. Regular aerobic and resistance exercise is associated with measurable reductions in CRP over weeks to months, particularly in people who start from an elevated baseline. Measuring CRP before and after a period of sustained exercise gives you objective evidence of whether your routine is reducing your inflammatory load. CRP is a general wellness marker, so persistent elevations should be discussed with a healthcare provider.
