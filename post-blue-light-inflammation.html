Does Blue Light from Screens Cause Inflammation? What the Research Shows

Most conversations about evening screen time focus on sleep quality and eye strain. The inflammation angle is less discussed but increasingly supported by research linking nighttime light exposure to measurable changes in inflammatory markers, including CRP.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes, there is real and growing evidence. Evening blue light from screens and LED lighting suppresses melatonin and disrupts the circadian clock, both of which are connected to higher levels of systemic inflammation, including elevated CRP. A prospective cohort study of young adults found that those with higher bedroom light at night carried significantly elevated high-sensitivity CRP, and a pilot intervention that included reducing evening blue light produced a meaningful decrease in inflammation. Blue light is not the most powerful driver of inflammation, but it is a genuinely modifiable one, and the mechanism is well understood.

It is easy to dismiss concerns about screen time as moral panic over modern technology. The legitimate worry, though, is not about screens being inherently harmful but about the timing of a specific kind of light signal hitting the eye at the wrong moment in the day. The body has an internal clock that expects darkness after sunset. When that clock receives bright, blue-enriched light at 10 p.m. from a phone or laptop, it does not simply ignore it. It interprets the signal as daytime and responds accordingly, with consequences that extend well beyond sleep disruption into the regulation of the body's inflammatory state.

Blue light refers to the portion of the visible spectrum spanning roughly 380 to 500 nanometers. It has the highest energy of any visible wavelength. Screens, LED lighting, and compact fluorescent bulbs all emit substantial amounts of it, particularly in the 460 to 480 nm range where biological effects are most pronounced.

Why Blue Light Has an Outsized Effect on the Body's Clock

The eye contains a class of specialized cells that are essentially dedicated to tracking light intensity over the course of the day. These are the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, often abbreviated to ipRGCs. Unlike the rods and cones that handle vision, ipRGCs do not form images. Their job is to report ambient light conditions to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the small brain structure that serves as the body's master circadian clock. ipRGCs are maximally sensitive to blue light at around 480 nm, which is precisely the wavelength that dominates the blue sky at midday. When these cells fire, the SCN receives a "it is daytime" signal and suppresses the pineal gland's release of melatonin.

This system works beautifully in a natural light environment where screens do not exist. As the sun sets and the sky shifts from blue to warm orange and red, ipRGC stimulation drops, the SCN shifts into nighttime mode, and melatonin begins rising about two hours later. The problem introduced by modern lighting is that it delivers blue-rich light at any hour the person chooses to look at a screen or sit under LED bulbs. A 2026 review in Chronobiology International noted directly that "modern urbanization has introduced chronic exposure to artificial light at night, particularly blue light from digital devices, which suppresses melatonin and desynchronizes central and peripheral clocks," and linked this desynchronization to amplified oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways (Deb et al., 2026).

How the Circadian Clock Normally Keeps Inflammation in Check

Inflammation does not operate at a steady level around the clock. CRP, IL-6, TNF-alpha, and other inflammatory markers all follow natural 24-hour rhythms that are coordinated by the circadian clock. These rhythms serve a purpose: the immune system is more active during the period corresponding to wakefulness, when the body is most likely to encounter infection or injury, and quieter at night when repair and restoration take priority. Research on shift workers, whose circadian clocks are chronically misaligned with the light-dark cycle, consistently finds higher rates of systemic inflammation and chronic inflammatory disease, a pattern that makes sense once you understand how tightly the clock and the immune system are coupled.

Circadian disruption appears to flatten or distort these inflammatory rhythms, often in a pro-inflammatory direction. When the timing of signals is off, the daily suppression of inflammatory activity that should happen during the nighttime window does not occur reliably. The result is not a dramatic spike in inflammation but a persistent low-grade elevation that accumulates over weeks and months. A 2026 review on cardiac fibrosis and circadian dysregulation described how loss of circadian regulation amplifies TGF-beta signaling, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling at the cellular level, connecting the daily light environment directly to changes inside cells that drive chronic disease (Deb et al., 2026).

A 2026 review on melatonin and artificial light across pregnancy research reinforced the same picture from a different angle. Analyzing clinical and animal studies, the review found that exposure to artificial light at night reduces melatonin production and is associated with adverse metabolic outcomes, with animal studies demonstrating causal effects including impaired offspring development, inflammation, and altered hormonal rhythms. The authors concluded that reducing evening light and screen exposure, maintaining regular sleep schedules, and optimizing nighttime darkness are practical circadian hygiene measures worth implementing (Cosendey et al., 2026).

What Human Studies Show About Light at Night and CRP

The most direct human evidence comes from a prospective cohort study of young adults that actually measured the amount of light inside participants' bedrooms at night. Using a portable illuminance meter, researchers from Anhui Medical University recorded real-ambient bedroom light levels in 276 participants (average age 18.7) and cross-referenced them with fasting blood samples measuring high-sensitivity CRP. The result was clear: higher bedroom nighttime light exposure was significantly associated with elevated hs-CRP. The association was particularly strong in physically inactive participants. For those meeting at least two hours per day of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, the effect was no longer statistically significant, suggesting that regular exercise can partially counteract the inflammatory toll of sleeping in a bright room (Xu et al., 2024).

A follow-up arm of the same study went deeper by recruiting 20 participants for 24-hour time-series blood sampling, with 10 from the low-light group and 10 from the high-light group. The researchers measured eight inflammatory markers every four hours over a full day and analyzed the circadian rhythms of each. Those in the high-light group not only had higher average inflammatory markers but also showed disrupted circadian rhythmicity, including altered amplitude and phase of inflammatory cytokines. The conclusion the authors drew was direct: keeping the bedroom dark at night may represent an important strategy for the prevention of chronic inflammation (Xu et al., 2024).

An intervention study published in 2026 offered a complementary piece of evidence from a different angle. Researchers at the University of Illinois Chicago enrolled eight adults with type 2 diabetes and diabetic retinopathy in a six-week behavioral intervention called Amplify-RHYTHM, which combined sleep stability coaching with timed light exposure, specifically morning bright light and evening blue light reduction along with a nightly light-blocking sleep mask. High-sensitivity CRP fell significantly after the intervention, from a mean of 2.36 to 1.58 mg/L (p = 0.011). While this was a small pilot without a control group and multiple components of the intervention changed simultaneously, the magnitude and statistical significance of the CRP change is notable and consistent with the mechanistic story (Reutrakul et al., 2026).

Why Melatonin Is Central to the Inflammation Story

Melatonin is not simply the hormone that makes you sleepy. It is one of the body's most broadly distributed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory molecules. Melatonin receptors are found throughout the immune system, and melatonin directly modulates inflammatory signaling at the molecular level. It inhibits NF-kappa-B, the master transcription factor that switches on the genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-alpha. It also scavenges reactive oxygen species directly, reducing the oxidative stress that is one of the primary triggers of inflammatory cascades. When melatonin production is robust and timed correctly, it functions as a nightly anti-inflammatory reset. When it is suppressed by artificial light, that reset does not happen.

This mechanism is not speculative; it is well documented across both animal and human research. The connection between light-induced melatonin suppression and downstream inflammation has been studied in contexts ranging from shift work to cancer biology to metabolic disease. In each case, the same chain appears: disrupted melatonin rhythm leads to altered circadian timing leads to dysregulated immune function leads to chronic low-grade inflammation. The question is not whether the connection exists but how large the effect is in a typical adult getting a few hours of screen time before bed, which is a harder question that the existing evidence cannot yet answer precisely.

The Timing Question: Why Evening Exposure Is What Matters

Not all blue light is bad for inflammation. This point is easy to miss and worth making clearly. Bright, blue-enriched light during the morning and daytime is actually beneficial for circadian health. It sharpens the contrast between the light and dark phases of the day, which makes nighttime melatonin production more robust, not less. The body needs a strong light signal in the morning to anchor the circadian clock to the solar cycle. Getting morning sunlight or sitting near a bright window is consistent with good circadian hygiene.

What creates the problem is blue-rich light during the evening hours when the clock expects darkness. Research on melatonin suppression shows that exposure to screens or bright LED lighting after sunset, especially in the two to three hours before bedtime, is when the signal is most disruptive. The photoreceptors responsible for circadian entrainment are most sensitive to light during this transition window, so a relatively low amount of blue light from a phone held a foot from the face can produce significant melatonin suppression that would not occur from the same source in the middle of the afternoon. The issue is not screens per se but screens at the wrong time.

Practical Steps to Reduce Evening Light Exposure

The most effective change is also the simplest: dim your environment after sunset. This means switching to warmer-toned bulbs in living spaces, reducing overhead lighting in favor of lamps positioned away from your line of sight, and not using very bright screens in dark rooms, where the contrast amplifies the signal to the eye. The goal is not to live in candlelight but to remove the strong blue-rich signal that tells the SCN it is still daytime.

Software filters and device settings can meaningfully shift the light quality from screens. Applications like f.lux, the iOS Night Shift feature, Android's Night Light, and similar tools shift the screen color temperature toward warmer, amber tones in the evening by reducing blue channel output. These are not perfect solutions, partly because they do not eliminate all blue light and partly because screen brightness itself contributes to the stimulating effect. Dimming the screen in addition to warming the color is more effective than color shifting alone.

Blue light blocking glasses are a popular tool, and the evidence for their usefulness is plausible but limited when it comes specifically to inflammation. They do reduce short-wavelength light reaching the ipRGCs, and several studies have shown improved sleep outcomes with their use. The direct evidence linking blue light blocking glasses to lower CRP or other inflammatory markers in healthy adults does not yet exist as a well-powered randomized trial. They are a reasonable precaution for people who must use screens late at night and cannot otherwise reduce exposure.

Bedroom darkness deserves particular attention. The Chinese cohort study measured ambient bedroom light during sleep, not screen use before bed. This matters because it shows that background light in the room where you sleep, from streetlights through thin curtains, standby lights on electronics, or a TV left on, is enough to shift inflammatory markers. Blackout curtains and removing glowing electronics from the bedroom address a source of light exposure that people often overlook entirely.

How Light Fits into the Broader Inflammation Picture

Evening blue light is not the dominant driver of inflammation for most people. The variables that move inflammatory markers the most are body composition, diet quality, sleep duration, physical activity, smoking, and chronic stress. These are orders of magnitude more powerful than the effect of a lamp in the bedroom or an hour of phone use before sleep. Someone who eats well, exercises regularly, maintains a healthy weight, does not smoke, and manages stress will have a healthy inflammatory profile regardless of their nighttime screen habits.

What the light evidence adds is a genuinely modifiable variable that most people have not thought about. The changes required are low-cost and low-effort: warmer bulbs, dimmer evenings, a darker bedroom. The Chinese cohort study's finding that physical activity can partially counteract the inflammatory effect of bedroom light is also encouraging, because it means these factors interact. Building a healthy lifestyle across multiple dimensions creates a system that is more resilient to any single stressor, including nighttime light.

CRP is a useful tool for tracking whether lifestyle changes are working. Because it reflects the body's overall inflammatory state, it can register improvements from multiple directions at once, including sleep quality, diet changes, exercise, and yes, changes to your light environment. It does not tell you which variable moved the needle, but it tells you whether the needle is moving. Because CRP is a general wellness marker rather than a diagnostic test, persistent elevations are always worth discussing with a healthcare provider. Tracking the trend over time, especially as you make changes, gives you data you would not otherwise have.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does blue light from screens cause inflammation?

Yes, there is real evidence of a link. Evening blue light from screens and LED lighting suppresses melatonin and disrupts the circadian clock, both of which are connected to higher levels of systemic inflammation. A prospective cohort study found that people with more light in their bedrooms at night had significantly higher hs-CRP, and an intervention that included reducing evening blue light produced a measurable decrease in CRP levels.

How does blue light affect CRP levels?

The main pathway is through the circadian clock. Blue light at night stimulates specialized eye cells that signal the brain's master clock to suppress melatonin. Melatonin has direct anti-inflammatory properties, including inhibiting NF-kappa-B signaling. When melatonin production is suppressed by evening blue light, this nightly anti-inflammatory effect is reduced, and the circadian rhythms of inflammatory markers like CRP and IL-6 become disrupted, often in a pro-inflammatory direction.

Does using night mode on your phone reduce inflammation?

Night mode and similar blue light filter features shift the screen color toward warmer tones by reducing blue channel output, which does reduce the circadian stimulation reaching the eye. Whether this translates to lower CRP in healthy adults has not been tested in a controlled trial. It is a reasonable precaution, and dimming the screen in addition to warming the color is more effective than color shifting alone. The bigger gains come from starting screen-free time earlier in the evening.

What time should you stop using screens to protect your inflammatory health?

The research on melatonin suppression suggests that the two to three hours before your habitual bedtime are the most sensitive window, because this is when the circadian clock transitions toward nighttime mode and is most responsive to light signals. Reducing bright screen use in this window, keeping overhead lighting dim, and ensuring the bedroom is as dark as possible during sleep are the changes most likely to protect the circadian-inflammatory system.
