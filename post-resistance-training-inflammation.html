Does Resistance Training Reduce Inflammation? What the Evidence Shows

Resistance training is well known for building muscle and improving metabolic health, but a growing body of meta-analyses shows it is also one of the most reliably effective lifestyle tools for lowering CRP and other inflammatory markers. The key is understanding how much, how often, and for whom the effect is strongest.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes. Multiple meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials show that regular resistance training produces meaningful reductions in C-reactive protein (CRP), the standard blood marker of systemic inflammation. The effect is consistent across age groups and health conditions, though it is strongest when training is performed at least three times per week, at an intensity of at least 70 percent of one-repetition maximum, and sustained for at least 12 weeks. People with elevated baseline inflammation, including older adults and those with type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome, tend to show the largest benefits.

Resistance training is usually associated with muscle building, strength gains, and weight management. Its relationship to inflammation is less often discussed, yet the research on this topic is now substantial enough to call it one of the more reliable lifestyle levers for lowering CRP. Understanding how lifting weights affects the inflammatory system, what the trial data actually shows, and what practical dose seems to work best gives people a more complete picture of why resistance training belongs in any serious conversation about managing chronic inflammation.

Resistance training refers to exercise in which muscles contract against an external force, typically free weights, machines, resistance bands, or body weight. It encompasses strength training, weight training, and power training, and is distinct from aerobic or cardiovascular exercise, though the two are frequently combined.

How Resistance Training Talks to the Immune System

When muscle contracts forcefully, it releases a cascade of signaling proteins called myokines. These are sometimes described as making skeletal muscle an endocrine organ, one that actively communicates with the immune system, the liver, the brain, and fat tissue. The most studied myokine is interleukin-6 (IL-6). During and immediately after a bout of resistance exercise, IL-6 is released in large quantities from contracting muscle fibers, causing a sharp but short-lived spike in blood levels. This might initially seem pro-inflammatory, but the context is critical: exercise-derived IL-6 is functionally different from the IL-6 that fat tissue produces continuously in people with obesity. The acute exercise-induced spike in IL-6 triggers the release of two potently anti-inflammatory signals, interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), which then actively suppress tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) and other chronic pro-inflammatory signals. According to a review by Nimmo MA et al. in Diabetes, Obesity and Metabolism, single bouts of exercise have a potent anti-inflammatory influence, and the interleukin-6 system appears central to mediating these effects (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24003921).

Body composition is the second major mechanism connecting resistance training to lower CRP. Visceral fat, the deep abdominal fat surrounding the liver and other organs, is metabolically active in a damaging way. It continuously secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-alpha and adipokines that keep CRP chronically elevated. Resistance training reshapes body composition over time by building lean muscle mass and reducing fat mass, particularly visceral fat, even when total body weight changes little. Each shift from fat to muscle tissue reduces the circulating background level of inflammatory signals. This is why the anti-inflammatory effect of resistance training builds gradually over weeks and months, rather than appearing after a single workout, and also why the effect is especially pronounced in people who carry more visceral fat.

Insulin sensitivity links resistance training, body composition, and inflammation in a reinforcing loop. Chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance form a vicious cycle: inflammation impairs insulin signaling, and insulin resistance drives fat accumulation and yet more inflammatory signaling. Skeletal muscle is the primary site of insulin-stimulated glucose uptake in the body. Larger, better-trained muscles are more metabolically responsive, pulling more glucose out of the bloodstream and reducing the need for the compensatory insulin spikes that stress tissues and amplify inflammation. By increasing muscle mass and improving insulin sensitivity simultaneously, resistance training disrupts this cycle from both ends, which is likely why the anti-inflammatory effects are especially pronounced in people with type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome.

What the Meta-Analyses Show

The clearest signal comes from older adults, who carry a heavier baseline inflammatory load and therefore show the most consistent benefit. A meta-analysis by Sardeli AV et al. published in Experimental Gerontology in 2018 pooled randomized controlled trials testing the effects of resistance training on inflammatory markers in adults over 50. The analysis found a statistically significant reduction in CRP (standardized mean difference = -0.61; 95% CI: -0.83 to -0.31; p < 0.001), along with a tendency toward reduced IL-6 and no significant change in TNF-alpha. Crucially, the CRP reduction was statistically significant only in trials combining at least three sessions per week, more than eight exercises per session, and training periods longer than 12 weeks, pointing to dose and duration as important moderators (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30071283).

A large 2025 and 2026 dose-response analysis sharpened these findings considerably. Qipo O et al. published a systematic review and meta-analysis in Ageing Research Reviews examining 36 randomized controlled trials. The analysis confirmed that resistance training significantly reduced pro-inflammatory biomarkers as a group, including CRP, TNF-alpha, IL-6, and IL-1beta, with an overall effect size of -0.66 (95% CI: -0.80 to -0.52; p < 0.001). The dose-response analysis found more pronounced effects with higher training frequency (at least three times per week) and higher intensity (at least 70 percent of one-repetition maximum), establishing a clear gradient: more demanding programs deliver stronger anti-inflammatory results (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41086999).

Among people with type 2 diabetes, the effect is even larger. A 2025 meta-analysis by Wang J et al. in Diabetes Research and Clinical Practice pooled 43 randomized controlled trials covering 2,012 adults aged 50 and older with type 2 diabetes and found a significant reduction in CRP (standardized mean difference: -0.80), alongside improvements in insulin resistance markers, muscle mass, and upper and lower body strength. That effect size is notably larger than the -0.61 seen in unselected older adults, consistent with the idea that people carrying heavier inflammatory loads, as many people with type 2 diabetes do, have the most to gain from resistance training (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41106502).

The picture is more nuanced in younger people with obesity-driven inflammation. A 2022 meta-analysis by Zhao H et al. in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health examined 14 studies in adolescents with obesity. It found that resistance training alone did not produce statistically significant reductions in IL-6 or CRP in this group, while aerobic training alone and combined aerobic plus resistance training both did reduce these markers. Combined training was the most effective modality overall. This finding is a useful qualifier: in younger people whose inflammation is primarily driven by excess body fat rather than by aging-related mechanisms, the aerobic component may deliver a more essential anti-inflammatory signal, and resistance training functions best as part of a combined program rather than as the sole intervention (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36293806).

How Much, How Often, and How Intense

Three sessions per week appears to be the threshold where anti-inflammatory effects become reliable. Across the reviewed meta-analyses, trials using once or twice weekly training consistently showed smaller or non-significant effects on CRP compared with those using three or more sessions per week. This likely reflects the need for sufficient total volume of myokine signaling and accumulated training stimulus to drive the body composition and metabolic adaptations that underlie lower baseline inflammation.

Intensity matters, and the data points to at least 70 percent of one-repetition maximum as a meaningful threshold. Programs using lighter loads performed for more repetitions did not show the same degree of CRP reduction as programs pushing into the moderate-to-high intensity range. This threshold is not so high that it requires elite training status. For most adults, 70 percent of one-repetition maximum corresponds to a weight they can lift for roughly 10 to 12 controlled repetitions before muscle fatigue sets in. It is well within reach for beginners who build up gradually and train consistently.

Duration of at least 12 weeks appears necessary for systemic CRP changes to become detectable. Short-term training blocks of 6 to 8 weeks show smaller and less consistent effects on CRP than programs lasting 12 weeks or more. This aligns with the biological logic: the pathways through which resistance training lowers CRP, particularly body composition changes and improved insulin sensitivity, are slow adaptations that take months to consolidate. Expecting CRP to drop after a few weeks of lifting is like expecting a diet to lower cholesterol in days. The mechanisms are real, but they operate on a longer timescale than acute responses.

Multi-joint, compound exercises involving multiple large muscle groups may deliver a stronger anti-inflammatory stimulus than single-joint isolation movements. The 2018 Sardeli meta-analysis found that significant CRP reductions required more than eight exercises per session, which is easier to achieve with programs built around compound movements such as squats, deadlifts, rows, and presses that recruit multiple muscle groups at once, compared with isolation exercises targeting single muscles. The more muscle tissue that is recruited during a session, the larger the total myokine signaling response, and the more metabolic work the session performs.

Combining Resistance and Aerobic Exercise

The evidence consistently suggests the combination of resistance and aerobic training outperforms either modality alone for inflammation outcomes. This is true across age groups, though it is especially clear in younger populations where resistance training alone may not be sufficient to move CRP significantly. The two modes of exercise work through partially distinct pathways: aerobic exercise particularly improves cardiovascular fitness, reduces visceral fat through higher caloric expenditure, and produces sustained post-exercise anti-inflammatory signaling; resistance training builds muscle mass and improves insulin sensitivity in ways that aerobic exercise does so less directly. Together, the pathways are additive.

For practical purposes, this means resistance training is most valuable as part of a broader physical activity pattern rather than as a standalone substitute for other movement. The ideal program for most people managing elevated CRP combines two to three resistance training sessions per week with moderate aerobic activity most other days. Neither component needs to be intense or prolonged to contribute. The consistent, long-term accumulation of training load is more important than any individual session.

Who Benefits Most

People with elevated baseline inflammation benefit most from resistance training for inflammation. This is not just a theoretical point. The meta-analyses reviewed above consistently found larger CRP reductions in populations that started with higher average CRP levels. If your CRP is already in the low-risk range below 1 mg/L, the absolute reduction from resistance training may be modest. If your CRP is persistently elevated above 3 mg/L, particularly in the context of excess visceral fat, pre-diabetes, or metabolic syndrome, the effect size from a well-dosed resistance training program is likely to be clinically meaningful.

Older adults carry the strongest evidence for benefit. The combination of age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia), increasing visceral fat accumulation, and what researchers call inflammaging (the gradual upward drift in baseline inflammation that occurs with healthy aging) creates a situation where resistance training addresses several problems at once. The case for resistance training in midlife and beyond is unusually strong precisely because the inflammatory, metabolic, and musculoskeletal benefits all point in the same direction.

People with type 2 diabetes or insulin resistance represent another group where the evidence is particularly compelling. The bidirectional relationship between inflammation and insulin resistance means that resistance training, by improving insulin sensitivity, also attacks the metabolic source of inflammation rather than just suppressing its downstream markers. This mechanistic depth makes the anti-inflammatory effect more durable than approaches that lower CRP without addressing the underlying metabolic dysfunction.

Where Resistance Training Fits in an Anti-Inflammatory Strategy

Among lifestyle interventions with solid randomized controlled trial evidence for reducing CRP, resistance training belongs in the same tier as dietary quality, sleep, weight management, and smoking cessation. Unlike pharmacological approaches, it also delivers benefits across multiple other domains simultaneously, including bone density, glucose regulation, cognitive function, and cardiovascular resilience. No single intervention covers as many inflammatory and metabolic risk factors at once, which is why exercise guidelines from major health organizations increasingly emphasize resistance training alongside aerobic activity rather than treating it as optional.

Tracking your CRP over a training block can help you see whether your program is working. Because individual responses vary considerably, and because the mechanisms are gradual, monitoring CRP at baseline and again after 12 to 16 weeks of consistent training provides concrete, motivating feedback. Some people see meaningful reductions within 12 weeks; others may need longer, or may find that gains require pairing resistance training with dietary changes or modest weight loss to become visible in the numbers. CRP is a general wellness marker rather than a diagnosis, and persistent elevations are always worth discussing with a healthcare provider. But for people trying to understand whether their lifestyle changes are moving the needle on inflammation, longitudinal CRP data is one of the most useful signals available.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does resistance training reduce CRP?

Yes. Multiple meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials show that regular resistance training produces statistically significant reductions in C-reactive protein (CRP), the standard marker of systemic inflammation. The effect is most reliably seen with at least three sessions per week, intensity at or above 70 percent of one-repetition maximum, and training lasting at least 12 weeks. Older adults and people with elevated baseline inflammation, such as those with type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome, tend to show the largest reductions.

How does resistance training reduce inflammation?

Resistance training reduces inflammation through several interacting mechanisms. Contracting muscles release anti-inflammatory myokines, particularly a form of interleukin-6 that triggers the release of IL-10 and IL-1Ra, which suppress pro-inflammatory signals such as TNF-alpha. Over time, resistance training also reduces visceral fat, one of the main drivers of chronic inflammatory signaling, and improves insulin sensitivity, which breaks the cycle between insulin resistance and inflammation.

How long before resistance training lowers CRP?

Most randomized controlled trial evidence suggests that meaningful reductions in CRP require at least 12 weeks of consistent training, with some studies finding larger effects after 16 to 24 weeks. The mechanisms, body composition changes, improved insulin sensitivity, and accumulated myokine adaptations, are gradual by nature. Consistency and patience over months are more important than the intensity of any single session.

Is resistance training or cardio better for inflammation?

Both reduce inflammation, and several meta-analyses suggest the combination of resistance training and aerobic exercise produces the strongest anti-inflammatory effects, outperforming either modality alone. Resistance training appears particularly effective in older adults and people with metabolic conditions, while aerobic exercise may deliver a stronger independent signal in younger populations with obesity-driven inflammation. For most people managing elevated CRP, a program combining both is the most evidence-backed approach.
