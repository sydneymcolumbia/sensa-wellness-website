Diverticulitis and Inflammation: When Gut Pouches Become a Problem

Small pouches in the colon are extremely common with age and usually cause no trouble at all. The difference between quiet diverticulosis and painful diverticulitis comes down to one thing: inflammation.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Diverticula are small pouches that bulge outward from the wall of the colon. Having them is called diverticulosis, and it is very common with age, usually causing no symptoms. Diverticulitis is what happens when one or more of those pouches becomes inflamed or infected, producing pain, fever, and a rise in inflammatory markers like CRP. By the age of 60, most people in high-income countries have developed small pouches in the wall of the colon, and the majority will never know it. These pouches, called diverticula, are one of the most common structural changes in the aging digestive tract. For the large majority of people they are completely silent. For a minority, though, one of the pouches becomes inflamed, and a quiet anatomical quirk turns into a painful, sometimes serious medical problem.

The dividing line between the harmless version and the problem version is inflammation. Understanding how a pouch goes from quiet to inflamed helps explain why fiber, the gut microbiome, and body-wide inflammatory signaling all show up repeatedly in the research on this condition.

What Diverticula Are and Why They Become Common With Age

Diverticula form where the colon wall is naturally weakest, at the points where small blood vessels pass through the muscular layer. When pressure inside the colon rises, the inner lining can push outward through these weak spots, creating a small balloon-like sac. Most are only a few millimeters to a centimeter across, and they tend to cluster in the sigmoid colon, the S-shaped segment on the lower left side where pressures are highest.

Prevalence climbs sharply with age. Diverticulosis is uncommon before 40 but extremely common after 60, and by 80 the majority of people have some diverticula. This age pattern reflects the slow, cumulative nature of the changes involved: the colon wall loses some of its elasticity over the decades, and repeated pressure gradually shapes the pouches.

It is usually silent. The important thing to hold onto is that having diverticula is not, by itself, a disease. Most people with diverticulosis live their entire lives without a single symptom. The pouches only matter clinically when inflammation enters the picture.

How Diverticulosis Turns Into Diverticulitis

The transition from quiet pouches to active diverticulitis is fundamentally an inflammatory event. For years the standard explanation was that a small piece of stool or debris became lodged in a pouch, blocked it, and caused a localized infection. That mechanical picture is now considered incomplete. Current thinking gives a much larger role to low-grade inflammation and the community of microbes living in the gut.

Chronic low-grade inflammation sets the stage. Research increasingly describes diverticular disease as having a smoldering inflammatory component in the colon wall even between acute attacks, more like the persistent immune activation seen in other gut inflammatory conditions than a purely mechanical plumbing problem. Nerve and immune-cell changes in the affected segment appear to be part of the story, which helps explain why some people have ongoing symptoms without a full-blown flare.

An acute flare is a burst of inflammation. When diverticulitis strikes, the pouch and surrounding tissue become acutely inflamed. Immune cells flood the area, pro-inflammatory signaling ramps up, and the body mounts a systemic acute-phase response. This is why blood tests during a flare typically show a rise in C-reactive protein and often an elevated white blood cell count. In more serious cases the inflammation can progress to a small abscess or a micro-perforation of the colon wall, which is why suspected flares need medical attention rather than watchful waiting at home.

The Role of the Gut Microbiome and Low Fiber

Two of the most active areas of research on diverticulitis converge on the same place: what you eat and the bacteria that eat it.

Fiber shapes colon pressure and stool bulk. A low-fiber diet produces smaller, harder stools, which require the colon to generate higher pressures to move contents along. Over time, those elevated pressures are thought to contribute to pouch formation and may make existing pouches more prone to trouble. This is the long-standing basis for the association between low fiber intake and diverticular disease, and large cohort studies have consistently linked higher fiber intake, particularly from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, with lower risk of diverticulitis. Our deeper dive on dietary fiber and inflammation covers how this works at the level of gut bacteria.

The microbiome may tip the balance. Fiber is also the primary food source for beneficial gut bacteria, which ferment it into short-chain fatty acids like butyrate. Butyrate nourishes the cells lining the colon and has direct anti-inflammatory effects on the gut wall. Research suggests that people with diverticular disease may have shifts in microbial composition, sometimes described as a reduction in these protective, fiber-fermenting bacteria. The working hypothesis is that a fiber-poor diet and a less favorable microbiome reinforce each other, creating an environment where inflammation is more likely to take hold. This overlaps with broader strategies for reducing inflammation in the digestive tract.

Myth-Busting: The Old Nuts and Seeds Advice

For decades, patients with diverticulosis were told to avoid nuts, seeds, popcorn, and corn, on the theory that these small, hard particles could lodge in a pouch and trigger diverticulitis. It was intuitive, widely repeated, and, as it turns out, not supported by the evidence.

Large prospective studies that followed people over many years found no increased risk of diverticulitis among those who ate nuts, seeds, popcorn, or corn. If anything, the data pointed toward these foods being part of the kind of high-fiber, plant-forward eating pattern associated with lower risk. Major gastroenterology guidance has since moved away from routinely recommending avoidance of these foods.

  • Nuts, seeds, popcorn, and corn are not off-limits for most people with diverticulosis, based on current evidence.
  • The bigger lever is total fiber, which supports softer stools, lower colon pressure, and a healthier microbiome.
  • During an acute flare, advice differs. Clinicians often recommend a temporary low-fiber or clear-liquid approach while the colon settles, then a gradual return to fiber. This is a short-term treatment measure, not a lifelong restriction, and it should be guided by a healthcare provider.

Lifestyle Factors Linked to Lower Risk

No single habit guarantees you will avoid diverticulitis, but research has consistently associated a cluster of modifiable factors with lower risk. These overlap heavily with general anti-inflammatory living, which is not a coincidence given the inflammatory nature of the condition.

  1. Eat more fiber. Higher intake of fiber from fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains is among the most consistent dietary associations with lower diverticulitis risk. Increase it gradually and with adequate fluids to let the gut adapt.
  2. Stay physically active. Regular activity, and vigorous activity in particular, has been associated with lower risk in cohort studies. Movement supports healthy bowel motility and is broadly anti-inflammatory.
  3. Maintain a healthy weight. Higher body weight, especially central adiposity, has been linked to greater risk of diverticulitis and its complications, consistent with the pro-inflammatory signaling that accompanies excess adipose tissue.
  4. Do not smoke. Smoking has been associated with a higher risk of diverticulitis and complications, adding to the long list of reasons to avoid it.
  5. Be mindful of certain medications. Frequent use of some pain relievers, particularly NSAIDs, has been associated in observational research with a higher risk of diverticular complications. This is worth discussing with a clinician if you use them regularly.

Because diverticular disease shares its inflammatory roots with many other chronic conditions, these same habits show up again and again across the research on gut and immune-mediated disease. The through-line is that keeping systemic inflammation low tends to be good for the colon as well.

Tracking Inflammation Over Time

C-reactive protein has a well-established role in the clinical picture of diverticulitis. During a suspected flare, doctors often measure CRP alongside a physical exam and imaging, because it rises with the acute-phase response and helps gauge how much inflammation is present and how a case is responding to treatment. That is a diagnostic use carried out by a clinician, and it is different from the everyday wellness tracking that a tool like Sensa is built for.

As a general wellness marker, CRP is most useful watched as a trend over time rather than as a one-off number. Sensa is designed to make that simple, letting you check your CRP levels at home without a needle or a clinic visit, so a measurement becomes a routine data point rather than a rare event. Watching your CRP hold low, or drift downward as you build the higher-fiber, more active, non-smoking pattern associated with lower diverticulitis risk, is a tangible signal that your lifestyle changes are moving in a helpful direction. CRP is a general wellness marker, not a diagnosis, so persistent elevations are worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

It is worth being clear about limits. Diverticulitis is a medical condition, and an acute flare is not something to self-manage with an at-home marker. If you develop severe or worsening abdominal pain, fever, or other symptoms of a flare, seek prompt medical care. At-home CRP tracking is a way to support healthy, anti-inflammatory habits over the long run, not a substitute for evaluation when something is acutely wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between diverticulosis and diverticulitis?

Diverticulosis is simply having diverticula, small pouches that bulge out through weak spots in the colon wall. It is very common with age and usually causes no symptoms. Diverticulitis is when one or more of those pouches becomes inflamed or infected, which produces pain, fever, and a rise in inflammatory markers like CRP. The dividing line between the two is inflammation.

Do nuts and seeds cause diverticulitis?

Current evidence says no. For decades people with diverticulosis were told to avoid nuts, seeds, popcorn, and corn on the theory that particles could lodge in a pouch, but large prospective studies found no increased risk of diverticulitis from eating these foods. Major gastroenterology guidance has moved away from routinely recommending avoidance, and these foods can be part of a healthy high-fiber diet.

How does fiber affect diverticulitis risk?

A low-fiber diet produces smaller, harder stools, which require the colon to generate higher pressures and may contribute to pouch formation and inflammation. Higher fiber intake from fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains is consistently linked with lower diverticulitis risk in large cohort studies. Fiber also feeds beneficial gut bacteria that produce anti-inflammatory short-chain fatty acids like butyrate.

Can I use an at-home CRP test to manage diverticulitis?

No. Diverticulitis is a medical condition, and an acute flare with severe abdominal pain or fever needs prompt medical care, not self-management. Doctors do measure CRP during flares as part of diagnosis and monitoring, but that is a clinical use.
