Is Bipolar Disorder Linked to Inflammation? What CRP Research Shows

Bipolar disorder has long been understood as a disorder of mood and brain chemistry. A growing body of research adds another layer to that picture: people with bipolar disorder tend to carry more low-grade inflammation, and their inflammatory markers shift with the phase of illness.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes, bipolar disorder is consistently associated with low-grade inflammation. Multiple meta-analyses have found that people with bipolar disorder have higher blood levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and pro-inflammatory cytokines than healthy individuals. The elevation is largest during manic episodes, present but smaller during depression and stable (euthymic) periods, and tends to decline when an acute episode resolves. Inflammation is not the sole cause of bipolar disorder, but it appears to be an active part of its biology. CRP is a general wellness marker that reflects this inflammatory burden, not a diagnostic test for any psychiatric condition.

For most of the twentieth century, bipolar disorder was framed almost entirely in terms of neurotransmitters, dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine, and the brain circuits they regulate. That framework remains central, but it has never fully explained why bipolar disorder so often travels alongside physical illnesses like cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. Over the past two decades, researchers have increasingly looked to the immune system for part of the answer, and the evidence linking bipolar disorder to chronic inflammation has become difficult to ignore.

This is part of a broader shift in psychiatry sometimes called immunopsychiatry, which studies how immune signaling influences mood, cognition, and behavior. Bipolar disorder sits near the center of that work, alongside depression, anxiety, and PTSD.

Bipolar disorder is a chronic mental health condition marked by episodes of mania or hypomania (elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with increased energy) alternating with episodes of depression and periods of relative stability known as euthymia. It affects roughly 1 to 2 percent of the global population.

What the Research Shows About CRP and Bipolar Disorder

C-reactive protein is the most widely studied inflammatory marker in bipolar disorder because it is cheap, stable, and reflects the activity of interleukin-6 (IL-6), a key upstream inflammatory signal. When researchers pool the available studies, a clear pattern emerges.

CRP is elevated across the mood spectrum, and highest in mania. A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis published in The Lancet Psychiatry, covering 27 studies and 2,161 people with bipolar disorder, found that CRP was most substantially increased during mania (Hedges' g 0.87) and moderately raised during depression (g 0.67) and euthymia (g 0.65) compared with healthy controls (Fernandes et al., 2016). In other words, inflammation is detectable even when mood is stable, but it intensifies during acute mania.

The elevation appears independent of medication. An earlier meta-analysis of 11 studies and 1,618 participants, published in The Journal of Clinical Psychiatry, confirmed that overall CRP levels were significantly higher in bipolar disorder than in controls and reported that the elevation was unrelated to the use of lithium or antipsychotic medication (Dargel et al., 2015). That matters because it suggests the inflammation is tied to the illness itself rather than simply being a side effect of treatment.

CRP often falls when an episode resolves. The same Lancet Psychiatry analysis found that CRP concentrations decreased after the resolution of an acute manic episode, suggesting that inflammatory activity tracks, at least in part, with the state of the illness rather than remaining fixed.

Beyond CRP: The Cytokine Evidence

CRP is a downstream marker. To understand what is driving it, researchers look at cytokines, the signaling proteins immune cells use to coordinate inflammation. Here, too, the bipolar literature is substantial.

Multiple cytokine systems are activated. A meta-analysis of 30 studies and 2,599 participants published in Biological Psychiatry found significantly elevated concentrations of pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, and regulatory cytokines in bipolar disorder, including IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha), and the soluble receptors for IL-2 and IL-6 (Modabbernia et al., 2013). The involvement of both pro- and anti-inflammatory signals suggests a dysregulated immune response rather than simple one-directional inflammation.

Some markers shift with mood phase. A later systematic review and meta-analysis in The British Journal of Psychiatry, synthesizing 53 studies, reported that no single biomarker reliably distinguished mood phase on its own, but that combinations of markers, such as high-sensitivity CRP together with IL-6, tended to differ by phase (Rowland et al., 2018). This is why researchers increasingly talk about inflammatory signatures rather than any one magic marker.

Why Would Inflammation and Bipolar Disorder Be Connected?

The association is robust, but association alone does not explain mechanism. Researchers have proposed several overlapping pathways, and the relationship is almost certainly bidirectional.

Inflammatory signals reach the brain. Cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-alpha can influence the brain through several routes, affecting the production and recycling of neurotransmitters, activating the brain's resident immune cells (microglia), and promoting oxidative stress. A review in The Psychiatric Clinics of North America argued that dysfunction of the innate immune system may play a genuine role in the pathophysiology of bipolar disorder, and noted that lithium, a first-line mood stabilizer, has measurable anti-inflammatory properties of its own (Rosenblat and McIntyre, 2016).

Mood episodes are physiologically stressful. Mania and depression both activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system, disrupt sleep, and alter eating and activity patterns. Each of these can independently raise inflammatory markers, which may help explain why CRP rises during acute episodes and settles afterward.

Shared lifestyle and metabolic factors amplify the loop. People living with bipolar disorder have higher rates of smoking, disrupted sleep, physical inactivity, and obesity, all of which are themselves pro-inflammatory. Inflammation and the illness can therefore reinforce one another, with each making the other somewhat worse over time.

What This Means for Physical Health

One of the most important reasons researchers care about inflammation in bipolar disorder is that it may help explain a sobering statistic: people with bipolar disorder have a substantially shorter average life expectancy than the general population, driven largely by cardiovascular disease rather than by the psychiatric condition itself.

Chronic low-grade inflammation is a well-established contributor to heart disease, atherosclerosis, and insulin resistance. If bipolar disorder carries a persistent inflammatory burden even between mood episodes, as the euthymic CRP data suggest, then that burden could be one of the threads connecting the mental and physical sides of the condition. This reframes inflammation not as a curiosity of brain science but as a shared risk factor worth taking seriously for whole-body health.

Can Reducing Inflammation Help?

This is an active and still-unsettled area of research, and it is important to be careful here. Anti-inflammatory treatments are not an established therapy for bipolar disorder, and nothing about the inflammation link changes the central role of psychiatric care, mood stabilizers, and professional monitoring.

That said, several adjunctive anti-inflammatory approaches have been studied as add-ons to standard treatment, including omega-3 fatty acids, N-acetylcysteine, and certain anti-inflammatory medications, with mixed and preliminary results. The broader and better-supported point is that the same lifestyle factors known to lower inflammation generally, regular physical activity, better sleep, a less processed diet, and not smoking, also support cardiovascular and metabolic health, which is exactly where people with bipolar disorder face elevated risk. These are complements to psychiatric care, never replacements for it.

Where CRP Tracking Fits In

CRP cannot diagnose bipolar disorder, identify a mood episode, or substitute for clinical judgment. What it can do is provide a simple, objective window into the body's overall inflammatory load, the same load that the research links to both mood-episode activity and long-term cardiovascular risk.

Because CRP in bipolar disorder appears to be elevated even during stable periods and to shift with the course of illness, it is the kind of marker that is most informative when watched as a trend rather than read once. C-reactive protein responds to the lifestyle levers, sleep, activity, diet quality, and smoking status, that also matter for physical health. Sensa is designed to make checking CRP at home simple, turning a measurement into a routine data point rather than a rare clinic event. Watching whether your CRP holds low or drifts upward over months can be a tangible part of a broader wellness picture, and any persistent elevation is worth discussing with a healthcare provider, especially for anyone managing a chronic condition.

The inflammation research does not rewrite what bipolar disorder is, but it does widen the frame. It positions the condition as something that involves the whole body, not just the brain, and it offers a measurable, modifiable dimension, inflammation, that connects mental and physical health in a way that was underappreciated a generation ago.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does bipolar disorder cause high CRP?

People with bipolar disorder tend to have higher CRP than healthy individuals, and pooled research shows the elevation is largest during mania, moderate during depression and stable mood, and tends to fall when an acute episode resolves. CRP is a general marker of inflammation, not a diagnostic test for bipolar disorder, and many other factors also raise it.

Is bipolar disorder an inflammatory condition?

Bipolar disorder is classified as a mood disorder, not an inflammatory disease, but strong evidence links it to immune system dysregulation and chronic low-grade inflammation. Researchers increasingly view inflammation as one active component of its biology rather than its sole cause.

Does treating inflammation help bipolar disorder?

Anti-inflammatory treatments are not an established therapy for bipolar disorder. Some adjunctive approaches such as omega-3 fatty acids and N-acetylcysteine have been studied as add-ons to standard care with mixed, preliminary results. Lifestyle factors that lower inflammation, such as exercise, sleep, diet quality, and not smoking, support the cardiovascular health where people with bipolar disorder face elevated risk, but they complement psychiatric care rather than replace it.

Why do people with bipolar disorder have more heart disease?

People with bipolar disorder have higher rates of cardiovascular disease and a shorter average life expectancy, driven in part by shared risk factors including chronic inflammation, metabolic changes, smoking, and disrupted sleep. The persistent low-grade inflammation seen even between mood episodes may be one of the threads connecting the mental and physical sides of the condition.
