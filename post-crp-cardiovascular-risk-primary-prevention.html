CRP and Heart Risk: New Research Shows Inflammation Predicts Cardiovascular Events Even When Cholesterol Is Normal

A major 2026 study of more than 416,000 UK Biobank participants finds that high-sensitivity CRP predicts heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular death across every level of baseline cardiovascular risk, including among people who would not qualify for treatment under conventional cholesterol-based guidelines.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes. A 2026 analysis of 416,619 UK Biobank participants published in Atherosclerosis found that high-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) independently predicted major adverse cardiovascular events across all risk groups, from people considered low-risk by standard calculators to those already in secondary prevention. The study adds to a growing body of evidence suggesting that checking CRP alongside cholesterol can reveal heart risk that lipid panels alone would miss, and it supports the idea that inflammation is a genuinely independent contributor to cardiovascular disease. CRP is a general wellness marker, and elevated levels are always worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

Most people who get a cardiovascular checkup hear about their cholesterol. Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides: these are the numbers that drive treatment decisions in clinics around the world. But cholesterol tells only part of the story. Research spanning more than two decades has consistently shown that roughly half of all heart attacks occur in people whose LDL cholesterol falls within the normal range. Something else must be contributing to their risk. A growing body of evidence points to chronic low-grade inflammation, measured in the blood as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), as a critical missing piece of the puzzle.

A 2026 study published in Atherosclerosis now adds some of the most detailed evidence to date. By following more than 416,000 people across five different cardiovascular risk categories and tracking them for a median of more than 15 years, researchers found that hsCRP independently predicted major cardiac events at every level of risk. The finding reinforces calls for broader hsCRP measurement as part of routine cardiovascular assessment, and it has immediate practical implications for anyone who has been told their cholesterol is "fine."

High-sensitivity CRP (hsCRP) is a blood marker that detects even small increases in C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases in response to inflammatory signals. While a standard CRP test flags acute illness, hsCRP measures the low-grade chronic inflammation associated with cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and other conditions. Levels below 1 mg/L are considered lower risk, 1 to 3 mg/L average risk, and above 3 mg/L higher relative risk for cardiovascular events.

What the New UK Biobank Study Found

The study, led by Tucker and colleagues and published in October 2026, analyzed 416,619 UK Biobank participants who had no prior atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease at enrollment. Participants were divided into five groups based on their baseline cardiovascular risk: 241,010 classified as low-risk, 74,179 as high-risk, 25,512 as very high-risk, 60,177 who were already taking lipid-lowering therapy, and 15,720 in secondary prevention (meaning they had survived a cardiovascular event and were being managed to prevent a second one). The researchers then followed each group to see who experienced major adverse cardiovascular events, defined as a first heart attack, ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular death (Tucker et al., 2026).

The core finding was striking in its consistency: hsCRP was directly and significantly associated with major adverse cardiovascular events in both men and women across every single risk group. The statistical signal held at p less than 0.001 in all subgroups, meaning it was extremely unlikely to be a chance result. Higher hsCRP was linked to more cardiac events, and that relationship did not disappear when the researchers adjusted for the standard components of the SCORE2 cardiovascular risk model, including age, sex, smoking, blood pressure, and cholesterol. In other words, hsCRP was adding information that traditional risk factors were not capturing.

The strength of the association varied across risk groups, with the most dramatic effect in secondary prevention. Among people already in secondary prevention, those in the highest quartile of hsCRP had a hazard ratio for major cardiovascular events that was 79% higher in men and 81% higher in women compared to those in the lowest quartile. That is a substantial difference. But even in the low-risk group, those with high hsCRP faced meaningfully greater risk than those with low hsCRP, challenging the assumption that someone with no traditional risk factors has little to worry about.

The authors concluded that their findings support selective measurement of hsCRP to refine risk stratification beyond what conventional tools provide. They note that randomized trials are still needed to establish whether acting on hsCRP measurements, for example by targeting inflammation as a treatment goal, actually reduces hard outcomes. But as an observational signal in one of the world's largest health databases, the data are hard to ignore.

Why CRP Predicts Risk Even in "Low-Risk" People

The biology behind this finding comes down to what CRP actually measures. Atherosclerosis, the buildup of plaque in artery walls that underlies most heart attacks and strokes, is now understood to be an inflammatory process from start to finish. Inflammatory cells called macrophages infiltrate the arterial wall, engulf oxidized LDL cholesterol, and become what are known as foam cells. This process triggers local inflammatory signaling, which recruits more immune cells, destabilizes plaques, and can ultimately cause the rupture that blocks blood flow and triggers a heart attack. CRP is a downstream marker of this systemic inflammatory state, produced by the liver in response to cytokines like interleukin-6 that are released during this process.

Crucially, this inflammatory activity can be ongoing for years before cholesterol levels climb into ranges that trigger clinical concern. A person may have a relatively modest LDL level but significant arterial inflammation driving plaque development and instability. Their standard cardiovascular risk score, which is built around cholesterol and blood pressure, will not capture this. hsCRP can. This is why studies consistently find that adding hsCRP to risk models improves their accuracy, particularly for people in the intermediate and lower risk categories who are most likely to be untreated.

Low-grade systemic inflammation has multiple sources beyond the arteries themselves. Excess visceral fat, poor sleep, sedentary behavior, a diet high in ultra-processed food, chronic psychological stress, gum disease, and subclinical infections all contribute to the background inflammatory tone that drives up hsCRP. Someone with normal cholesterol can easily accumulate multiple such contributors, pushing their chronic inflammation, and by extension their cardiovascular risk, well above what their lipid panel suggests. This is the physiological explanation for why heart attacks happen in people who seemed "fine" on paper.

How This Builds on the JUPITER Trial

The 2026 observational data echoes a landmark randomized controlled trial that established inflammation's role in cardiovascular prevention more than 15 years ago. The JUPITER trial, published in The New England Journal of Medicine in 2008, enrolled 17,802 apparently healthy men and women who had LDL cholesterol below 130 mg/dL but hsCRP at 2.0 mg/L or higher. Because their cholesterol was not elevated, these people did not meet the criteria for statin therapy under standard guidelines at the time. The trial randomly assigned them to 20 mg of rosuvastatin daily or placebo and followed them until cardiovascular events occurred (Ridker et al., 2008).

The trial was stopped early because the benefit of treatment was so clear. Rosuvastatin reduced hsCRP by 37% and LDL cholesterol by 50%, and the rate of major cardiovascular events fell by 44% (hazard ratio 0.56) compared with placebo. Heart attacks fell by 54%, stroke by 48%, and deaths from cardiovascular causes by 47%. The trial demonstrated that people with elevated inflammation but normal cholesterol faced real cardiovascular risk, and that an intervention reducing both LDL and CRP provided substantial protection. The JUPITER results are part of the reason cardiologists began paying closer attention to hsCRP as a clinical tool.

The 2026 Tucker et al. study extends the JUPITER logic into real-world primary prevention at much larger scale. While JUPITER was a randomized trial that established the concept in a selected population over a relatively short follow-up, the UK Biobank analysis is a population-level observational study with more than 15 years of follow-up and a sample covering the full spectrum of cardiovascular risk. Together, these two lines of evidence, one experimental and one observational, tell a coherent story: inflammation, as captured by hsCRP, is a genuine and independent contributor to cardiovascular risk, and knowing about it changes what we know about who is actually at risk.

The Thresholds That Matter

Cardiology organizations have used a simple three-tier classification of hsCRP for more than two decades. Values below 1 mg/L indicate lower relative cardiovascular risk; values between 1 and 3 mg/L indicate average risk; and values above 3 mg/L indicate higher relative risk. These thresholds were established from large prospective studies and are used in clinical risk assessment tools such as the Reynolds Risk Score, which incorporates hsCRP alongside traditional factors to improve cardiovascular risk prediction, particularly for women and for people at intermediate risk on standard calculators.

What the new research clarifies is that the relationship is continuous, not just categorical. Within any given risk category, people with higher hsCRP do worse than those with lower hsCRP. This means the question is not simply "am I above or below 3 mg/L" but rather "where am I in the distribution, and is my level trending up or down over time?" A reading of 2.8 mg/L and a reading of 0.6 mg/L are both technically in the "average risk" range, but they represent meaningfully different positions, and a trend from 0.6 to 2.8 over two years is more informative than any single snapshot.

Several lifestyle factors are well documented to lower hsCRP and, in doing so, may reduce the risk the new study links to elevated levels. Weight loss in people with excess body fat produces one of the most reliable reductions in CRP, as does regular aerobic exercise. A dietary pattern emphasizing vegetables, fruit, legumes, fish, and whole grains while reducing ultra-processed food, added sugar, and refined carbohydrates consistently moves CRP downward in clinical trials. Quitting smoking, improving sleep duration and quality, and managing chronic stress all contribute. None of these changes require a prescription, and monitoring hsCRP over time can help you see whether the changes you are making are actually working.

What This Means for Wellness Monitoring

The practical implication of this growing body of evidence is that cholesterol alone is an incomplete picture of cardiovascular health. Understanding where your inflammation level sits adds a dimension that standard lipid panels cannot provide. For someone who has been told their cholesterol is normal and given reassurance, knowing their hsCRP is persistently elevated at, say, 3.5 mg/L should prompt a conversation with a healthcare provider about what might be driving it and what they can do about it.

At-home hsCRP testing makes this kind of monitoring practical for the first time. Rather than waiting for an annual physical and hoping the question comes up, individuals can now check their own CRP levels, track trends over time as they make lifestyle changes, and bring meaningful longitudinal data to their healthcare conversations. CRP is a general wellness biomarker, not a diagnostic test, and it does not diagnose or treat any medical condition. But as the 2026 Tucker study underscores, knowing your number has genuine value: it reflects the cumulative inflammatory burden your cardiovascular system is carrying, independent of what your LDL is doing.

The growing research on CRP and primary prevention suggests we are moving toward a more complete model of heart risk. The question is no longer just "how high is your cholesterol?" but "how much systemic inflammation are you carrying, and what can you do about it?" The answer to that second question turns out to matter a great deal, even when the first answer is reassuringly normal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can CRP predict heart disease if my cholesterol is normal?

Yes. A 2026 UK Biobank study of more than 416,000 people found that high-sensitivity CRP predicted major cardiovascular events, including heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular death, across all cardiovascular risk groups, including people considered low-risk by standard calculators. The earlier JUPITER trial showed that people with normal LDL but elevated hsCRP faced real cardiovascular risk and benefited substantially from statin therapy. These findings suggest that cholesterol alone provides an incomplete picture of heart health.

What is a concerning hsCRP level for cardiovascular risk?

Cardiologists commonly use three risk tiers: hsCRP below 1 mg/L indicates lower relative cardiovascular risk, 1 to 3 mg/L indicates average risk, and above 3 mg/L indicates higher relative risk. Readings above 10 mg/L typically suggest acute inflammation or infection rather than chronic cardiovascular risk. Because the relationship is continuous, even within these tiers someone at the high end faces greater risk than someone at the low end, and tracking your trend over time is as informative as any single reading. Persistent elevations are worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

What lifestyle changes can lower hsCRP and cardiovascular risk?

Several lifestyle changes have solid evidence for reducing hsCRP. Regular aerobic exercise, weight loss in people with excess body fat, a diet low in ultra-processed food and added sugar, and one rich in vegetables, fish, and whole grains are among the most effective. Quitting smoking, improving sleep, and managing chronic stress also reduce systemic inflammation. These are general wellness steps that can lower inflammatory burden and may reduce the cardiovascular risk associated with elevated CRP, and they are worth pursuing independent of any medication decisions.

Should I get my CRP tested even if I have no symptoms?

Many people with elevated CRP feel completely healthy, which is why researchers describe chronic low-grade inflammation as "silent." If you are interested in understanding your inflammatory burden as part of a general wellness picture, an hsCRP test can be informative. It is particularly worth discussing with a healthcare provider if you have multiple cardiovascular risk factors, a family history of early heart disease, or if standard risk calculators put you at borderline risk. CRP is a general wellness marker, not a diagnostic test for any disease, and elevated results should be discussed with a qualified clinician.
