Does Acupuncture Reduce Inflammation? What the Research Shows

Acupuncture is one of the oldest therapies still in wide use, and modern research has started to measure what it does to the inflammatory signals circulating in your blood. The answer is more interesting, and more nuanced, than either fans or skeptics tend to admit.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Possibly, to a modest degree. Several meta-analyses of randomized trials find that acupuncture, especially electroacupuncture and ear (auricular) acupuncture, is associated with lower levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and TNF-alpha. The effect appears real but is inconsistent across studies, and acupuncture works best as a complement to proven anti-inflammatory habits rather than a replacement for them. It is a wellness practice, not a treatment for any disease.

Acupuncture, the practice of inserting thin needles at specific points on the body, has been used in traditional East Asian medicine for more than two thousand years. For most of that history, any effect on inflammation was invisible, because inflammation itself could not be measured. That has changed. Researchers can now draw blood before and after a course of acupuncture and quantify the exact molecules the immune system uses to turn inflammation up or down, including CRP, IL-6, and TNF-alpha.

What those measurements show is a therapy that genuinely seems to nudge inflammatory signaling, through plausible biological pathways, but with effects that vary a great deal depending on the technique, the condition, and the quality of the study. This article walks through the mechanisms, the human evidence, and the honest limitations, so you can decide where acupuncture fits, if anywhere, in a plan to keep chronic inflammation low.

What Inflammation Is and Why a Needle Might Touch It

Inflammation is the immune system's response to threat. In its acute form it is protective: blood vessels dilate, immune cells rush to an injury or infection, and the area becomes red, warm, and swollen while repair happens. The problem is chronic, low-grade inflammation, the kind that simmers for months or years without an obvious cause and contributes to conditions ranging from cardiovascular disease to metabolic dysfunction. This slow burn is reflected in a modestly elevated CRP and in persistently raised cytokines like IL-6.

The reason a needle could influence any of this is that inflammation is not purely a local chemical event. It is actively regulated by the nervous system. The vagus nerve, in particular, carries signals that can switch off cytokine production through what researchers call the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. If acupuncture stimulates the sensory nerves that feed into these circuits, it has a route to influence immune activity far from the needle itself. That is the core hypothesis behind most modern acupuncture-and-inflammation research.

How Acupuncture Could Lower Inflammation: The Neuro-Immune Pathways

Laboratory work over the past decade has mapped several ways acupuncture appears to act on inflammation, both locally at the needle site and systemically through the nervous system.

Local immune cell effects. According to a comprehensive 2021 review in the Journal of Inflammation Research, inserting a needle deforms connective tissue and prompts nearby cells to release signaling molecules that recruit and reprogram immune cells. One of the most consistent findings is that acupuncture encourages macrophages to shift from their pro-inflammatory "M1" state toward the anti-inflammatory, tissue-repairing "M2" state, and suppresses downstream NF-kappaB and MAPK pathways that drive cytokine production (Li et al., 2021).

The vagal and cholinergic route. Sensory signals from acupoints travel to the spinal cord, brainstem, and hypothalamus, where they can activate the vagus-adrenal axis and the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway. The net result in animal models is a release of neurotransmitters and hormones that tells immune cells to produce fewer inflammatory cytokines. This neuro-immune signaling is why the location and intensity of stimulation matter so much, and why electroacupuncture, which adds a small electrical current to the needles, often produces larger effects than manual needling alone.

Oxidative stress and resolution. The same review notes that acupuncture can enhance the body's antioxidant defenses, reducing the oxidative stress that fuels inflammatory cell infiltration, and may help the body resolve inflammation more efficiently once a threat has passed. These are the mechanisms that make a measurable drop in blood markers biologically plausible rather than magical.

What Human Studies Show About Acupuncture and Inflammatory Markers

Mechanisms are only a starting point. The more important question is whether acupuncture actually lowers inflammatory markers in people, and here the controlled trials give a cautiously encouraging, if uneven, picture.

Ear acupuncture has the clearest signal. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in Neuromodulation pooled 27 randomized controlled trials involving 1,122 participants and found that auricular stimulation, which includes auricular acupuncture and related ear-based techniques, significantly reduced circulating C-reactive protein, TNF-alpha, IL-6, and IL-10 compared with controls (Hua et al., 2025). The ear is densely supplied by a branch of the vagus nerve, which fits the cholinergic-pathway theory neatly.

Some short-term trials show large CRP drops. In a 2025 pilot randomized trial of 29 hospitalized patients with cellulitis, a skin infection, adding acupuncture to standard antibiotic care was linked to a faster fall in CRP: levels averaged 27.0 mg/L after four days in the acupuncture group versus 63.9 mg/L in the sham group (Shechter et al., 2025). This was a small study of an acute infection, not chronic inflammation, but it illustrates the direction of effect that keeps researchers interested.

But CRP does not always move. A 2026 randomized trial of 120 patients undergoing coronary stenting found that intraoperative electroacupuncture significantly lowered TNF-alpha and IL-6 compared with sham, yet produced no significant difference in high-sensitivity CRP (Liang et al., 2026). This matters: different inflammatory markers respond on different timescales, and CRP, which is produced by the liver in response to IL-6, can lag behind or fail to shift even when upstream cytokines fall. A single marker never tells the whole story.

Condition-specific evidence is growing. A 2025 network meta-analysis in Frontiers in Medicine that compared non-drug therapies for inflammatory bowel disease across 62 randomized trials reported that acupuncture and moxibustion ranked among the most effective options for inducing clinical remission and reducing serum TNF-alpha (Jia et al., 2025). Similar signals appear in studies of rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis, where acupuncture is most often used for pain.

Where the Evidence Is Weak

Enthusiasm needs to be tempered by the real limitations of this literature, which even supportive meta-analyses acknowledge.

Heterogeneity is high. Studies differ enormously in the points used, the number and length of sessions, the technique (manual versus electroacupuncture versus ear acupuncture), and the population studied. When results are this scattered, pooled averages can mask the fact that many individual trials show no effect at all.

Blinding is genuinely hard. It is difficult to create a convincing "fake" acupuncture control, because patients can often tell whether a needle penetrated the skin. Some trials use non-penetrating sham needles or non-acupoint locations, but sham acupuncture is not inert, which can either exaggerate or obscure a true effect. Many positive studies are also small and short, and small early trials tend to overstate benefits.

Much of the strongest mechanistic data is from animals. The elegant vagal and macrophage findings come largely from rodent experiments. They make the human results plausible, but they are not the same as proving a clinically meaningful drop in a person's chronic inflammation over months. The honest summary is that acupuncture appears to move inflammatory markers in the right direction, more reliably for some cytokines and conditions than others, but the effect size and durability in everyday use remain uncertain.

What This Means If You Are Trying to Lower Inflammation

If you are drawn to acupuncture, the reasonable way to think about it is as a possible complement to the interventions with the strongest and most consistent anti-inflammatory evidence, not a substitute for them. The foundations remain the same: an anti-inflammatory diet, regular physical activity, good sleep, not smoking, and stress management. These have large, repeatable effects on CRP and cytokines across thousands of participants.

Acupuncture may add value on top of that foundation, particularly if you are already using it for a condition like chronic pain, osteoarthritis, or tension headaches, where it has better-established symptomatic benefits. It is generally safe when performed by a licensed, trained practitioner using sterile single-use needles, with serious adverse events being rare. It is reasonable to view any effect on your inflammatory markers as a welcome bonus rather than the main event, and to keep your expectations proportional to the modest, uneven evidence.

Because Sensa is a general wellness tool, it is worth repeating that neither acupuncture nor CRP tracking diagnoses, treats, or cures any condition. If you have a specific inflammatory or autoimmune disease, acupuncture should be discussed with the clinician managing it, not used in place of proven care.

Tracking Your Inflammation If You Try Acupuncture

One of the frustrations with acupuncture, as with many lifestyle interventions, is that its effect on your inflammation has traditionally been invisible. You feel more relaxed after a session, but you cannot see whether your underlying inflammatory tone is actually changing. Measuring CRP over time is a way to replace that guesswork with data.

C-reactive protein is a practical marker to watch because it reflects the IL-6-driven signaling that acupuncture is thought to influence, and because it responds to your whole anti-inflammatory picture, not acupuncture alone. The key is sensible timing. Avoid testing in the days right after an acute illness, injury, or intense workout, any of which can temporarily raise CRP and muddy the trend. Instead, establish a baseline over a few consistent measurements, then re-check periodically as you add or adjust habits.

Sensa is designed to make that routine simple, letting you check CRP at home without a needle draw or a clinic visit, so a measurement becomes a regular data point rather than a rare event. If you decide to try a course of acupuncture, tracking your CRP trend before, during, and after gives you a personal readout of whether your inflammation is drifting down, holding steady, or unaffected, which is far more informative than how any single session made you feel. Persistent elevations, regardless of what you are trying, are worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

The bottom line: acupuncture is a plausible, generally safe practice that modern research links to modest reductions in several inflammatory markers, most convincingly with ear-based and electroacupuncture techniques. Treat it as a complement to the fundamentals, keep your expectations grounded in the uneven evidence, and let your own measured trend tell you whether it is doing anything for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does acupuncture lower CRP?

Sometimes. Several randomized trials and meta-analyses have found that acupuncture, especially ear-based and electroacupuncture techniques, is associated with reduced C-reactive protein and other inflammatory markers. However, the effect is inconsistent: some well-designed trials show CRP dropping while others show no change, in part because CRP is a downstream marker that can lag behind the upstream cytokines acupuncture seems to influence first.

How might acupuncture reduce inflammation?

Research points to a neuro-immune mechanism. Stimulating sensory nerves at acupuncture points can activate the vagus nerve and the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, signaling immune cells to produce fewer inflammatory cytokines. Locally, acupuncture appears to shift macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory state and suppress NF-kappaB signaling. Much of this mechanistic detail comes from animal studies, which make the human findings plausible but do not prove them.

Is electroacupuncture better than regular acupuncture for inflammation?

It may be. Adding a small electrical current to the needles, known as electroacupuncture, tends to produce stronger and more consistent effects on inflammatory markers in studies than manual needling alone, likely because it provides more sustained stimulation of the nerve pathways involved. Ear (auricular) acupuncture also shows a relatively strong signal, which fits with the ear's rich vagal nerve supply.

Can acupuncture replace medication or lifestyle changes for inflammation?

No. Acupuncture is best considered a possible complement to the interventions with the strongest anti-inflammatory evidence, such as diet, exercise, sleep, and not smoking, rather than a replacement for them or for prescribed medical care. If you have a diagnosed inflammatory or autoimmune condition, any complementary therapy should be discussed with your healthcare provider.
