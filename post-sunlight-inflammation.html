Does Sunlight Reduce Inflammation? What the Research Shows

The sun has a complicated relationship with your immune system. Moderate exposure appears to calm inflammation through several pathways, but the dose matters more than almost any other factor.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Moderate sunlight exposure appears to lower inflammation through at least three pathways: it triggers the skin to release nitric oxide that relaxes blood vessels, it drives vitamin D production that helps regulate the immune system, and it prompts regulatory immune cells that dampen overactive responses. Population data back this up, with inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) tending to run lower in summer than in winter. But the relationship is dose-dependent. Too much ultraviolet light does the opposite, igniting inflammation in the skin and accelerating aging, so the benefit lives in sensible, moderate exposure rather than deliberate tanning or burning.

Few environmental factors are as misunderstood as sunlight. For decades the public health message was simple and one-directional: the sun causes skin cancer, so cover up and stay out of it. That message saved lives, and the danger of excessive ultraviolet (UV) radiation is real. But a growing body of research has complicated the picture by uncovering a second story, one in which carefully dosed sunlight has measurable benefits for blood pressure, metabolism, and the chronic, low-grade inflammation that underlies so many modern diseases.

The result is a genuine biological paradox. The same spectrum of light that can inflame and age the skin can, at lower doses, help quiet the systemic inflammation circulating through the rest of the body. Understanding how to think about sunlight means holding both of those truths at once.

Nitric oxide (NO) is a short-lived signaling molecule that relaxes the smooth muscle in blood vessel walls, widening the vessels and lowering blood pressure. The skin stores large reserves of nitric oxide and its precursors, and ultraviolet light can release them into the circulation.

The Nitric Oxide Pathway: Sunlight Beyond Vitamin D

For years, researchers assumed that any health benefit of sunlight had to run through vitamin D. That assumption began to crack when scientists at the University of Edinburgh and the University of Southampton ran a careful experiment on the skin itself.

Sunlight lowers blood pressure through the skin. In a randomized study of 24 healthy volunteers, exposing the skin to UVA light lowered blood pressure, and it did so by mobilizing nitric oxide stored in the upper layers of the skin rather than through vitamin D, which UVA does not produce (according to PubMed, Liu et al., Journal of Investigative Dermatology, 2014, PMID 24445737). The effect appeared within minutes and was independent of the enzyme normally responsible for making nitric oxide, pointing to a pre-formed reservoir in the skin that light simply unlocks.

Why this matters for inflammation. Nitric oxide is not only a blood pressure regulator. It is a central messenger in vascular health, and impaired nitric oxide signaling is a feature of the endothelial dysfunction that accompanies chronic inflammation and cardiovascular disease. By improving blood vessel function and lowering pressure, sunlight-driven nitric oxide release appears to ease one of the mechanical and biochemical stresses that keep the vascular system in a low-grade inflammatory state. Reviews of the field now describe UV exposure as a plausible nonpharmacological lever against cardiovascular and metabolic disorders, acting largely through nitric oxide and, separately, through vitamin D (according to PubMed, Quan et al., Photodermatology, Photoimmunology & Photomedicine, 2023, PMID 37731181).

This nitric oxide story reframes an old debate. It suggests that at least part of sunlight's benefit is written into the skin itself, not borrowed from a single vitamin, and it helps explain findings that vitamin D supplements alone have struggled to reproduce.

Vitamin D: The Immune Regulator

The more familiar pathway is also real. When UVB light strikes the skin, it converts a cholesterol derivative into vitamin D3, which the body then activates into a hormone with wide-ranging effects on immune function.

Vitamin D helps calibrate the immune system. Immune cells carry vitamin D receptors, and the active hormone nudges the system toward a less inflammatory posture: it supports regulatory T cells, tempers the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and helps keep the innate immune response proportionate. Low vitamin D status has been repeatedly associated with higher inflammatory markers, although the direction of cause and effect is not always clean, since inflammation itself can lower circulating vitamin D. We cover the nutrient side of this story in more depth in our guide to vitamin D and inflammation.

Sunlight is the body's primary vitamin D source. Most people make far more vitamin D from sensible sun exposure than they get from food, which is one reason vitamin D status, and the inflammation that tracks with it, tends to dip in the darker months. That seasonal pattern shows up clearly in large inflammation datasets, discussed below.

Seasonal Patterns: What Population Data Reveal

If sunlight genuinely influences inflammation, you would expect inflammatory markers to rise and fall with the seasons. They do.

CRP runs lower in summer. In a study of more than 18,000 apparently healthy adults, average CRP was meaningfully lower in summer (1.51 mg/L) than in winter and spring (1.76 mg/L), and after adjusting for age, sex, body weight, and other risk factors, the odds of having an elevated CRP were roughly 26 percent higher in winter than in summer (according to PubMed, Sung, International Journal of Cardiology, 2006, PMID 16503255). The authors linked this seasonal inflammatory swing to the well-documented rise in heart attacks and strokes during winter months.

Not just vitamin D, not just cold. Seasonal inflammation is shaped by many overlapping forces, including temperature, winter infections, reduced physical activity, and dietary shifts. But sunlight threads through several of them. Work on the skin and blood pressure has suggested that a substantial share of the seasonal variation in blood pressure appears to track with ultraviolet exposure rather than temperature alone, consistent with the idea that the skin acts as a light-sensitive regulator of cardiovascular tone (according to PubMed, Johnson, Titze & Weller, Current Opinion in Nephrology and Hypertension, 2016, PMID 26625865).

Sunlight and the Immune System's Brakes

Beyond nitric oxide and vitamin D, ultraviolet light has a direct and somewhat surprising effect on immune cells: it activates the system's braking mechanisms.

UV expands regulatory T cells. Experimental work has shown that UV exposure can induce regulatory T cells that secrete interleukin-10 (IL-10), an anti-inflammatory signal, and that these cells can dampen overactive immune responses, including the kind of allergic, Th2-driven airway inflammation seen in asthma models (according to PubMed, Wang et al., Immunobiology, 2009, PMID 19450899). This immunoregulatory property is the same reason controlled UV phototherapy is a long-standing treatment for inflammatory skin conditions like psoriasis and eczema.

A double-edged mechanism. The very capacity of UV to suppress immune responses is also what makes overexposure dangerous, because a suppressed immune system is less able to catch and clear damaged cells in the skin. This is the hinge on which the entire sunlight question turns: the same immunoregulatory switch that can calm inappropriate inflammation can, pushed too hard, undermine healthy immune surveillance.

The Dose Problem: When Sunlight Inflames Instead

Everything above describes moderate exposure. The picture inverts at high doses, and this is where the old warnings remain entirely valid.

Too much UV drives skin inflammation and aging. Excessive ultraviolet radiation damages the DNA and proteins in skin cells, triggers a local inflammatory cascade, and over time pushes skin cells into a senescent, inflammation-secreting state. Researchers describe this process of UV-induced inflammation and immunosuppression as a driver of photoaging that closely mirrors the chronic, low-grade inflammation of biological aging, sometimes called inflammaging (according to PubMed, Salminen, Kaarniranta & Kauppinen, Inflammation Research, 2022, PMID 35748903). A sunburn is, quite literally, an acute inflammatory injury.

More is not better. This is the key takeaway that distinguishes evidence-based sun habits from wishful thinking. The benefits described in this article come from short, regular, non-burning exposure. The harms come from intense, prolonged, or burning exposure. The goal is a low, steady dose, not a maximal one, and that principle holds across skin tones, though people with darker skin produce vitamin D more slowly and people with very fair skin burn faster.

Putting It Into Practice

Translating this research into daily life does not require sunbathing or abandoning sun protection. A few sensible habits capture most of the benefit while respecting the real risks:

  • Get morning light. A short walk outdoors early in the day delivers daylight that both supports vitamin D (later in the day when UVB is stronger) and anchors your circadian rhythm, which independently governs inflammatory signaling.
  • Favor regular, brief exposure. Several short sessions of unprotected skin exposure spread across the week appear more useful, and far safer, than occasional long ones. Stop well before any pinkness appears.
  • Protect against burning, not against all light. Use shade, clothing, and sunscreen to prevent burns and during peak-intensity hours or extended time outdoors, rather than treating every minute of sunlight as a threat.
  • Combine it with time in nature. The benefits of being outdoors extend beyond UV. Spending time in green space is independently associated with lower stress and inflammation, as we discuss in our piece on nature and inflammation.
  • Mind the winter gap. In higher latitudes, UVB is too weak in winter to make meaningful vitamin D, which is part of why inflammatory markers drift up in the cold months. This is where diet, supplementation discussed with a provider, and other lifestyle levers matter most.

Tracking How Sunlight Affects Your Inflammation

Because the inflammatory effect of sunlight is modest and seasonal, it is the kind of influence that only becomes visible over time rather than in any single moment. That makes it a natural fit for trend tracking. C-reactive protein is the most practical marker to watch, since it reflects the same systemic, IL-6-driven inflammatory signaling that sunlight, vitamin D, and the seasons all nudge.

Sensa is designed to make checking CRP simple, letting you measure it at home without a needle or a clinic visit, so a reading becomes a routine data point rather than a rare event. Watching your CRP trend across the year can show you your own seasonal pattern and whether the lifestyle levers you are pulling, including sensible outdoor time, are bending that trend in the right direction. CRP is a general wellness marker rather than a diagnosis, so a persistent elevation is worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

The sun, it turns out, is neither villain nor miracle. It is a powerful biological input with a narrow optimal dose. Used with respect, moderate sunlight is one more modifiable factor that can help keep your baseline inflammation low, alongside sleep, movement, and the food on your plate.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does sunlight lower CRP?

Population studies find that C-reactive protein tends to run lower in summer than in winter, consistent with a seasonal, sunlight-linked effect. In one study of more than 18,000 healthy adults, average CRP was 1.51 mg/L in summer versus 1.76 mg/L in winter and spring. Sunlight is one of several seasonal influences, alongside temperature, activity, and infections, so it is not the only driver, but moderate exposure appears to push inflammatory signaling in a favorable direction.

How does sunlight reduce inflammation if not through vitamin D?

Sunlight works through more than one pathway. UVA light mobilizes nitric oxide stored in the skin, which relaxes blood vessels and lowers blood pressure independently of vitamin D. UVB light produces vitamin D, which helps regulate immune cells. And UV exposure can expand regulatory T cells that secrete the anti-inflammatory signal IL-10. The nitric oxide pathway helps explain why sunlight has benefits that vitamin D supplements alone have not fully reproduced.

Can too much sun increase inflammation?

Yes. The relationship is strongly dose-dependent. Excessive ultraviolet radiation damages skin cells, triggers an acute inflammatory response (a sunburn is an inflammatory injury), and over time promotes photoaging driven by chronic low-grade inflammation. The benefits described in the research come from short, regular, non-burning exposure, not from tanning or burning. More sun is not better.

How much sun exposure is beneficial?

Research points to short, regular, non-burning exposure rather than a single prescribed dose, since skin tone, latitude, and season all change how quickly the skin responds. A practical approach is brief sessions of outdoor light spread across the week, stopping well before any redness, combined with shade, clothing, or sunscreen during peak hours and longer stretches outside. In winter at higher latitudes, UVB is often too weak to make much vitamin D, so other habits matter more.
