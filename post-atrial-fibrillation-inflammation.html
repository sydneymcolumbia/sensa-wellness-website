Does Inflammation Cause Atrial Fibrillation?

Atrial fibrillation is the most common heart rhythm disorder, and a growing body of research places inflammation at the center of how it starts, persists, and comes back.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Inflammation does not act alone, but the evidence strongly ties it to atrial fibrillation (AFib). People with higher levels of inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) are more likely to develop AFib, more likely to have it return after treatment, and more likely to develop it after heart surgery. Inflammation appears to be both a contributor to AFib and a consequence of it, creating a self-reinforcing loop in the heart's upper chambers.

Atrial fibrillation is a condition in which the heart's two upper chambers, the atria, beat in a fast and disorganized way instead of a steady rhythm. It is the most common sustained arrhythmia worldwide and a leading cause of stroke, heart failure, and hospitalization. For decades, AFib was understood mainly in electrical and structural terms: faulty signaling, stretched chambers, scarred tissue. More recently, researchers have recognized that inflammation is woven through all of these processes.

The question is not simply whether inflammation and AFib occur together. It is whether inflammation helps cause AFib, and whether measuring it tells you anything useful about risk. On both counts, the research has become hard to ignore.

What the Research Actually Shows

The link between inflammation and AFib is one of the more consistently replicated findings in cardiology, spanning large population cohorts, meta-analyses, and surgical studies.

Inflammation predicts new atrial fibrillation. The landmark evidence comes from the Cardiovascular Health Study, a large population-based cohort. Among 5,806 adults followed for roughly seven years, those in the highest quartile of CRP had a 31 percent higher risk of developing new AFib compared with those in the lowest quartile (adjusted hazard ratio 1.31), and the association held even after accounting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors (Aviles et al., Circulation, 2003). At baseline, people in the highest CRP quartile were also nearly twice as likely to already have AFib (adjusted odds ratio 1.8). This was one of the first studies to show that inflammation was not just present alongside AFib but appeared to precede it.

The pattern holds across many studies. A meta-analysis pooling observational data found that elevated CRP, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha were each significantly associated with AFib risk, with CRP showing a standardized mean difference of 0.95 between people with and without AFib (Wu et al., International Journal of Cardiology, 2013). Notably, the association was strongest for persistent and permanent AFib and weaker for brief paroxysmal episodes, which fits the idea that sustained inflammation helps AFib entrench itself rather than simply flicker on and off.

Inflammation predicts recurrence. When doctors restore a normal rhythm using electrical cardioversion, AFib often returns. A meta-analysis of prospective studies found that patients with higher CRP before cardioversion were significantly more likely to slip back into AFib afterward (Liu et al., Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 2007). In other words, the inflammatory state of the heart appears to influence whether a normal rhythm will stick.

How Inflammation Drives the Arrhythmia

The association is strong, but mechanism is what turns a correlation into a plausible cause. Researchers have mapped several ways inflammation physically changes the atria in ways that favor fibrillation.

Structural remodeling and fibrosis. Chronic inflammation promotes fibrosis, the buildup of stiff scar-like tissue in the atrial walls. Fibrotic tissue conducts electrical signals unevenly, creating patches where impulses can loop and fragment instead of traveling smoothly. This electrical disorganization is the substrate on which AFib runs. Inflammatory signaling molecules such as IL-6 and TNF-alpha directly stimulate the cells that lay down this fibrotic tissue.

Electrical remodeling. Inflammatory cytokines alter the behavior of ion channels, the tiny gateways that control the electrical charge of heart cells. These changes can shorten the atrial refractory period, the brief window during which a cell cannot be re-triggered, making it easier for rapid, chaotic signals to take hold and sustain themselves.

Oxidative stress. Inflammation and oxidative stress travel together. Reactive oxygen species generated during inflammation damage atrial cells and interfere with the proteins that handle calcium inside heart muscle, further destabilizing the rhythm. This overlap is part of why conditions that raise inflammation, from obesity to high blood pressure to sleep apnea, also raise AFib risk.

A self-reinforcing loop. Here is the twist that makes AFib and inflammation so entangled: AFib itself appears to generate inflammation. The rapid, irregular contraction of the atria stresses the tissue and triggers an inflammatory response, which in turn promotes more remodeling and more AFib. This feedback loop helps explain why AFib tends to become more persistent over time, a phenomenon clinicians summarize as "AFib begets AFib."

The Clearest Example: Atrial Fibrillation After Surgery

If you wanted to design an experiment to test whether a burst of inflammation can trigger AFib, heart surgery comes close. Cardiac operations, especially those using cardiopulmonary bypass, provoke a powerful systemic inflammatory response. Post-operative AFib is one of the most common complications, affecting a substantial share of patients, and it lengthens hospital stays and raises the risk of other complications.

A meta-analysis of 15 studies including more than 3,000 patients found that higher CRP levels were associated with a greater likelihood of developing AFib after coronary artery bypass surgery (Li et al., American Journal of Therapeutics, 2016). A separate systematic review concluded that the inflammatory response to surgery is a genuine pathophysiologic driver of post-operative AFib, not merely a bystander, and that strategies to dampen that inflammation, including statin pre-treatment, showed promise for prevention (Anselmi et al., The Annals of Thoracic Surgery, 2009). The timing is telling: the inflammation peaks in the days after surgery, and AFib tends to appear in that same window.

What This Means If You Are Thinking About Your Own Risk

It is important to be precise about what the research supports. Elevated inflammation is a risk marker and a contributing factor for AFib, not a diagnosis, and a normal CRP does not rule out arrhythmia. Many people develop AFib for reasons that have little to do with inflammation, including genetics, valve disease, thyroid problems, and age. CRP is a general wellness marker, and interpreting heart rhythm symptoms is a job for a clinician with an ECG, not a single lab value.

What inflammation does offer is a modifiable lever. The same lifestyle factors that lower chronic inflammation, maintaining a healthy weight, treating sleep apnea, staying physically active, moderating alcohol, and managing blood pressure, are also the factors most consistently linked to lower AFib risk. This is not a coincidence. Many of these interventions appear to work in part by lowering the inflammatory load on the atria.

  • Weight and visceral fat: Excess fat tissue is metabolically active and secretes inflammatory signals. Weight loss has been shown to reduce AFib burden in people who are overweight.
  • Alcohol: Heavy and even moderate drinking raises both inflammation and AFib risk, and cutting back reduces episodes in many people.
  • Sleep apnea: Untreated obstructive sleep apnea is a potent driver of both inflammation and AFib, and treating it improves rhythm control.
  • Physical activity: Regular moderate exercise lowers baseline inflammation, though extreme endurance training can paradoxically raise AFib risk in some people.

Where Tracking CRP Fits In

Because inflammation is one of the threads connecting AFib to its risk factors, CRP offers a window into that underlying state. It will not tell you whether your heart is in rhythm at any given moment, and it is not a substitute for an ECG or a cardiologist. What it can do is reflect the chronic inflammatory load that the research links to arrhythmia risk, and show whether that load is trending up or down as you make changes.

C-reactive protein is the most practical marker for this because it reflects the same IL-6-driven inflammatory signaling implicated in atrial remodeling, and it responds within days to weeks to real change. Sensa is designed to make checking it simple, letting you measure CRP at home without a needle or a clinic visit, so a reading becomes a routine data point rather than a rare event. For someone working to lower their cardiovascular risk, watching CRP drift downward as they lose weight, improve sleep, or cut back on alcohol is a tangible signal that the inflammatory pressure on the heart is easing. CRP is a general wellness marker rather than a diagnosis, so persistent elevations, and any symptoms such as palpitations, breathlessness, or a fluttering heartbeat, are worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

The bottom line is that inflammation is not the whole story of atrial fibrillation, but it is a central and increasingly well-documented part of it, and unlike age or genetics, it is something you can measure and influence.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does inflammation cause atrial fibrillation?

Inflammation is strongly linked to atrial fibrillation and appears to be a genuine contributing factor, not just a coincidental finding. Higher levels of inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6 predict a greater risk of developing AFib, and inflammation physically remodels the atria through fibrosis and changes to electrical signaling. It is one of several drivers rather than the sole cause, and AFib itself also generates inflammation, creating a self-reinforcing loop.

Can a high CRP level mean I have AFib?

No. A high CRP reflects general inflammation in the body and is not a diagnosis of any heart rhythm problem. It signals elevated cardiovascular risk that is worth paying attention to, but atrial fibrillation can only be confirmed with an ECG or heart rhythm monitoring. If you have palpitations, a fluttering or irregular heartbeat, breathlessness, or dizziness, see a healthcare provider regardless of your CRP level.

Why does atrial fibrillation often happen after heart surgery?

Cardiac surgery, especially with cardiopulmonary bypass, triggers a strong systemic inflammatory response, and post-operative AFib commonly appears in the days afterward when that inflammation peaks. Studies have found that higher CRP levels are associated with a greater risk of AFib after bypass surgery, and research suggests that strategies which reduce inflammation, such as statin pre-treatment, may help prevent it.

Can lowering inflammation reduce AFib risk?

Lifestyle changes that lower chronic inflammation, including maintaining a healthy weight, treating sleep apnea, moderating alcohol, staying active, and controlling blood pressure, are also the changes most consistently linked to lower AFib risk. Many appear to work partly by easing the inflammatory load on the atria. These steps support general cardiovascular wellness but are not a treatment for diagnosed AFib, which requires medical care.
