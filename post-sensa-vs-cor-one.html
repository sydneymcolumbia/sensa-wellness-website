Sensa vs COR One: Two Ways to Track Inflammation at Home

Both let you check an inflammation marker at home without a lab visit. They differ on what they measure, how you collect the sample, how you pay, and where your data goes. Here is an honest side-by-side look.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Competitor facts verified on corhealth.com on October 27, 2026.

The short answer

Both are at-home inflammation trackers you read yourself without a lab, but they measure different markers with different samples and pricing models. COR One reads ESR from a drop of blood in about 30 minutes on a device plus a monthly membership. Sensa reads CRP from saliva in about 15 minutes with no device purchase and no subscription, at $49 per kit or less in multi-packs. Choose COR if you want a blood-based ESR reading and are happy with a subscription. Choose Sensa if you want a needle-free, no-subscription way to track your CRP trend.

If you want to check an inflammation marker at home without booking a blood draw, two products take very different routes to the same goal. COR One (COR One is a trademark of COR Health) is a countertop reader that measures erythrocyte sedimentation rate, or ESR, from a drop of blood. Sensa is a saliva kit that scores C-reactive protein, or CRP, using your phone camera. This comparison covers what each one measures, what testing is like, what a year of use costs, where your data goes, and how each company describes its regulatory status.

We verified COR's specifications and prices on corhealth.com on October 27, 2026. Where we could not confirm a detail, we attribute the claim to COR's website.

What Each One Measures: ESR vs CRP

ESR is a settling speed. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate measures how quickly red blood cells sink to the bottom of a thin tube over a fixed period. When inflammation is present, proteins such as fibrinogen make red cells stick together and fall faster. ESR has been used in clinics for roughly a century. It responds slowly, often taking days to rise and days to weeks to come back down, and it is influenced by factors unrelated to inflammation, including age, anemia, and pregnancy. COR describes ESR as reflecting "the combined effect of multiple inflammation-related proteins," which is why it is considered a broad marker.

CRP is a single protein with a fast clock. C-reactive protein is produced by the liver in response to inflammatory signals, mainly interleukin-6. It can rise within hours of a trigger and typically returns toward baseline within a few days once the trigger resolves. That speed makes CRP useful for tracking how your body responds to a change, such as a new sleep routine, a training block, or a cold. Sensa measures CRP in saliva and reports it as a 0 to 100 inflammation score, not as a blood concentration.

Neither one is a diagnosis. Both ESR and CRP are nonspecific. A high reading tells you inflammation is present somewhere; it does not tell you where or why. An at-home reading from either product is a data point for your own awareness and for a conversation with a provider, not a verdict. If you are weighing lab-grade CRP options, our guide to hs-CRP vs standard CRP explains the difference between the two blood assays.

Sample and Experience

COR One: finger prick, tube, countertop reader. According to COR's how-to-use page, you plug in the device, connect it to Wi-Fi, load a single-use Mini Test Tube into the Sample Station, trigger a lancet, and collect one drop of blood from your fingertip. The tube goes into the COR One, which contains a camera that photographs the sample periodically for 30 minutes. COR compares the prick to a glucose test and says it uses "the thinnest needle available for minimal discomfort." You will need a power outlet, a Wi-Fi network, and a supply of tubes.

Sensa: spit, wait, scan. Sensa is a single-use kit with no device to buy. You spit into the vial, wait for the color to change, and scan the vial with the Sensa app on your phone. The app turns the color reading into a 0 to 100 inflammation score and places it in one of three tiers: In range, Worth watching, or Time to reset. There is no needle, no reader to plug in, and nothing to charge. The trade-off is that saliva is a different fluid from blood, and Sensa's score is a wellness index rather than a laboratory concentration.

Household use. COR advertises Family Mode for several profiles on one device. Sensa kits are individual; each person scans their own vial in their own app account.

Time to Result

COR states "results in 30 minutes" across its site. The device analyzes the tube for the full half hour, then, per the how-to-use page, you receive an email with a link to view the result. Sensa's color change and scan take about 15 minutes from spit to score, and the score appears in the app as soon as the scan completes. Both are far faster than a lab draw, which commonly takes days. For most people the 15-minute gap will matter less than the other factors in this comparison.

Cost Over a Year

COR's pricing is a membership. On October 27, 2026, COR listed the COR One device at $299 outright, including five test tubes, and advertised that the device and Sample Station ("$299 value") are included free with an active membership. Membership was listed at $29 per month when billed annually (COR calls this a 26 percent savings) or $39 per month on month-to-month billing, with up to five tests per month and tubes auto-shipped. COR's product page also mentions additional tests "as low as $10 a test." If you stop, COR says you can "pause or cancel and send your device back."

Sensa's pricing is per kit. A single Sensa kit is $49. A three-pack is $99 (about $33 per test) and a four-pack is $129 (about $32 per test). Shipping is free, there is no subscription, and there is no device to buy. You pay only for the kits you use.

The arithmetic depends on how often you test. Using COR's annual-billing price of $29 per month, a year of COR membership is $348 and covers up to 60 tests. Here is how Sensa compares at a few frequencies, buying the cheapest pack combination:

  • Twice a year (2 tests): Sensa $98 with two single kits. COR $348.
  • Quarterly (4 tests): Sensa $129 for one four-pack. COR $348.
  • Monthly (12 tests): Sensa $387 for three four-packs. COR $348.
  • Weekly (52 tests): Sensa $1,677 for 13 four-packs. COR $348 on annual billing, or $468 on monthly billing.

The break-even point lands at about nine tests a year. Below that, Sensa costs less. At ten or more tests a year, COR's flat membership costs less per test, as long as you actually use the tests you are paying for. If you want a weekly data point, the membership is the cheaper path.

Data and Privacy

COR sends sample images to its servers. COR's how-to-use page explains that the device must be connected to Wi-Fi so it can transmit images of your blood sample to COR's servers, where the analysis happens. Results are then delivered by email link and in the COR Dashboard web app. Your sample images and results live in COR's cloud, and the device does not produce a result offline.

Sensa scans on your phone and stores results in your account. With Sensa, the phone camera reads the vial's color and the app records the score to your Sensa account so you can see your trend over time. We publish how we handle that data, including what we collect and how to delete it, on our consumer health data page. Either way, you are trusting a company with a health-related reading. Read both privacy policies and decide what you are comfortable with.

Regulatory Positioning

COR describes the COR One as "FDA-Registered." That phrase appears on COR's homepage and product page, alongside "Medical-Grade" and "FSA & HSA Eligible." Those are COR's descriptions of its own product, and we have not independently evaluated them. In the United States, registering with the FDA is a separate step from FDA clearance or approval; questions about which applies to any product belong with that company and the FDA's public databases.

Sensa is a general wellness product. Sensa is not an FDA-cleared, approved, or registered device, and we do not describe it as one. It is designed to support a healthy lifestyle by helping you see your CRP trend over time. It is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease, and a Sensa score is not a substitute for a lab test or a visit with a healthcare provider. If you need a CRP or ESR value for a clinical decision, ask your doctor for a lab order.

Who Each Is For

COR One fits you if you want a blood-based ESR reading, you plan to test often enough that a flat membership pays off, you are comfortable with a finger prick, and you do not mind a device that needs an outlet and Wi-Fi. Family Mode is a plus for households that want to share one reader.

Sensa fits you if you want a needle-free way to track CRP, you prefer paying per test with no recurring charge, you want a result in about 15 minutes with nothing to set up, or you are testing a few times a year to see how a lifestyle change is landing. It also suits people who want to try at-home inflammation tracking once before committing to anything.

Both can make sense for some people. ESR and CRP move on different timescales, and some people with inflammatory conditions track both with their doctor. For a broader look at the category, see our roundup of at-home CRP tests and our wellness device comparison.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is ESR the same as CRP?

No. ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) measures how fast red blood cells settle in a tube, which speeds up when inflammatory proteins make the cells clump. CRP (C-reactive protein) is a single protein the liver releases in response to inflammation. Both are nonspecific inflammation markers, but CRP rises and falls within hours to days while ESR moves more slowly and is also affected by age, anemia, and other factors. COR One measures ESR; Sensa measures CRP.

Does Sensa require a subscription?

No. Sensa is sold per kit with free shipping: $49 for a single kit, $99 for a three-pack, and $129 for a four-pack. There is no device to buy and no recurring charge. You spit into the vial, scan it with the Sensa app, and see a 0 to 100 inflammation score in about 15 minutes.

Which is more accurate, Sensa or COR One?

They measure different things, ESR from a drop of blood and CRP from saliva, so there is no direct accuracy comparison to make, and neither company publishes head-to-head data. Sensa is a general wellness product that reports a trend score rather than a laboratory concentration. For any clinical question, a lab test ordered by your doctor is the reference standard.

How much does COR One cost per year?

According to COR's website on October 27, 2026, membership is $29 per month billed annually ($348 per year) or $39 per month billed monthly ($468 per year), with up to five tests per month and the COR One device included while the membership is active. The device is also listed at $299 outright with five test tubes. Check corhealth.com for current pricing.

Can an at-home ESR or CRP reading diagnose a condition?

No. Both ESR and CRP are nonspecific inflammation markers. A reading can show that inflammation is present but cannot tell you the cause. Sensa is a general wellness product and is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease. If a reading concerns you or keeps trending up, talk with a healthcare provider, who can order the appropriate lab tests.
