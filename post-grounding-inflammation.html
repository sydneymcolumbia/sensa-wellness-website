Grounding and Inflammation: Does Earthing Really Work?

Walking barefoot on the earth is supposed to drain inflammation out of your body. The idea is appealing and the practice is harmless, but the science behind the claims is far thinner than the marketing suggests.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Grounding, also called earthing, is the practice of putting your bare skin in direct contact with the earth's surface in the belief that it lowers inflammation. The underlying hypothesis is intriguing but unproven. A handful of small pilot studies report subjective improvements, yet no large, rigorous trials confirm that grounding itself lowers inflammation. Barefoot time outdoors is pleasant and low-risk, but the habits with real evidence, activity, sleep, sunlight, and stress reduction, are found elsewhere.

Search for "grounding" or "earthing" and you will find confident claims that walking barefoot on grass, sand, or soil drains excess inflammation from your body, eases pain, and helps you sleep. You will also find a growing market of grounding mats, sheets, and patches sold on the same promise for people who cannot get outside. It is an appealing idea. Reconnecting with the earth sounds both natural and almost cost-free.

As inflammation writers, we think the honest thing to do is separate the appealing story from what the evidence actually supports. The claims deserve a fair hearing, and the underlying hypothesis is not absurd. But the gap between "plausible mechanism" and "proven benefit" is wide, and grounding sits firmly on the unproven side of it.

Grounding (or earthing) is the practice of making direct physical contact between your skin and the earth's surface, typically by standing or walking barefoot outdoors or by using a conductive mat wired to a grounded outlet. The central hypothesis is that the earth's surface carries a supply of free electrons that transfer into the body and neutralize inflammation-driving free radicals.

The Earthing Hypothesis, Stated Fairly

The core idea rests on real physics and real biology, at least at the edges. The surface of the earth carries a negative electrical charge and an abundance of free electrons. Free radicals, the reactive molecules produced during normal metabolism and amplified during inflammation, carry unpaired electrons that make them chemically aggressive and capable of damaging cells. The earthing hypothesis proposes that when your skin touches conductive ground, electrons flow into your body and pair off with those reactive molecules, neutralizing them and, over time, quieting the oxidative stress that helps sustain chronic inflammation.

Stated that way, it is not nonsense. Antioxidant chemistry really does involve donating electrons to neutralize free radicals, which is roughly how vitamin C and other dietary antioxidants are understood to work. The leap the hypothesis makes is that meaningful quantities of electrons can enter the body through the skin from the ground and travel to where inflammation is happening in amounts large enough to matter. That leap is where the evidence thins out dramatically, and it is the part that no study has convincingly demonstrated.

What the Studies Actually Show

The published grounding research is real, but it is small and shaky. Most studies enroll only a few dozen participants, sometimes fewer, and many were conducted or funded by people with a commercial or advocacy interest in the outcome. The typical design has people ground themselves during sleep or rest for a period of days or weeks, then reports changes in self-reported sleep, pain, mood, and occasionally a blood marker or two.

Some of these studies do report improvements, including in subjective sleep and pain and, in a handful of cases, in a measured inflammatory or immune marker. Taken at face value, that sounds encouraging. But the limitations are serious enough that the results cannot carry much weight:

  • Tiny samples. With only a handful of participants, ordinary variation and chance can easily produce a result that looks like an effect but would vanish in a larger group.
  • Weak or absent blinding. Many outcomes are subjective, and people generally know whether they are grounded. Expectation alone can improve reported sleep and pain, which is the textbook definition of a placebo effect.
  • Short duration. Chronic inflammation is a long-run phenomenon, and brief studies cannot tell you whether any change persists or matters over time.
  • Conflicts of interest and publication bias. Studies tied to product sellers, and a literature where positive results are more likely to be published, both tilt the picture toward benefit.

The fair summary is not that grounding has been disproven. It is that grounding has never been properly tested. There are no large, rigorous, independently run trials showing that grounding itself lowers inflammation, and until there are, the confident health claims are running far ahead of the science.

The Confounders Hiding in Plain Sight

Here is the problem that makes grounding studies so hard to interpret: the act of grounding almost never happens in isolation. Picture what "walking barefoot on grass" actually involves. You are outdoors. You are probably moving. You are in daylight. You are likely relaxed, away from a screen, and doing something you find pleasant. Every one of those things has a much stronger and better-established link to lower inflammation than skin contact with soil does.

So when someone grounds themselves in a park and later reports feeling better, we genuinely cannot tell whether the benefit came from the electrons or from the sunlight, the walking, the nature exposure, and the drop in stress that came bundled with it. This is the confounding problem, and it is not a minor quibble. It is the whole ballgame. The most likely explanation for any real benefit people experience from "earthing" is that the surrounding behaviors are doing the work, and the barefoot contact is along for the ride.

What Actually Has Evidence

The good news is that the behaviors bundled around grounding are exactly the ones with solid support, and you do not need a mat or a product to get them. If your goal is genuinely lower inflammation, this is where the evidence points:

  • Physical activity. Regular movement is one of the most reliably anti-inflammatory habits there is, and a barefoot walk outdoors counts. Exercise and inflammation is one of the best-documented relationships in the field.
  • Time in nature and daylight. Getting outside, especially in morning light, supports healthy circadian rhythms, and the circadian clock directly regulates inflammatory signaling. Nature exposure is also associated with lower stress and better mood.
  • Stress reduction. Chronic stress keeps cortisol and inflammatory signaling elevated, so anything that genuinely calms you down helps. See how stress drives inflammation for the mechanism.
  • Sleep. Poor sleep is a well-established driver of higher inflammatory markers, and if grounding while sleeping helps anyone, better sleep is the far more likely reason than electrons. Our piece on sleep and inflammation covers why.
  • Cold exposure and other hormetic stressors. Some popular practices have at least a plausible mechanism worth understanding on its own terms, as we discuss in cold exposure and inflammation.

Notice the pattern. Every proven lever is something grounding accidentally gives you by getting you outdoors and off the couch. That is a genuinely good reason to walk barefoot in the grass. It is just not the reason the earthing marketing gives you.

The Verdict on Grounding

So, does grounding work? For lowering inflammation specifically, the honest answer is that there is no good evidence it does anything on its own. The electron-transfer hypothesis is interesting but unproven, the supporting studies are too small and too flawed to trust, and the benefits people report are much more plausibly explained by the sunlight, movement, and calm that come with being outside.

That said, we are not here to talk anyone out of a harmless habit. Standing barefoot on the grass costs nothing, feels good, and pulls you toward genuinely beneficial behaviors. If you enjoy it, enjoy it. The distinction worth holding onto is this:

  • Barefoot time outdoors: pleasant, low-risk, and a nice on-ramp to activity and nature. No reason not to.
  • Grounding mats, sheets, and patches marketed as anti-inflammatory devices: not supported by strong evidence, often expensive, and promising a mechanism that has never been shown to work. Spend that money elsewhere.

Save your energy and your budget for the levers that are actually proven. If you want to reconnect with the earth, do it because it is enjoyable, not because a mat promises to drain your inflammation away.

Tracking Whether Any Habit Is Working

The reason grounding claims are so easy to sell is that most people have no way to check them. Without data, "I feel better" is all you have, and feeling better is exactly the outcome most vulnerable to placebo and expectation. This is where objective measurement changes the conversation, whether the habit in question is grounding, cold plunges, a new sleep routine, or anything else.

C-reactive protein is one of the most practical markers of general inflammation, and tracking your CRP levels over time turns a vague sense of "this works for me" into something you can actually see. Sensa is a general wellness tool designed to make that simple, letting you check CRP at home without a needle or a clinic visit, so a measurement becomes a routine data point rather than a rare event. If you want to test whether a habit genuinely moves your inflammation, watching your CRP trend across weeks and months is far more informative than any single reading or any product testimonial.

CRP is a wellness marker, not a diagnosis, and a single number tells you little on its own. The value is in the trend. And because persistent elevations can signal something that warrants medical attention, they are worth discussing with a healthcare provider rather than trying to solve with a grounding mat.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does grounding actually lower inflammation?

There is no strong evidence that grounding lowers inflammation on its own. A few small pilot studies report subjective improvements in sleep and pain and occasional changes in a marker, but the samples are tiny, the methods are weak, and no large, rigorous trials confirm the effect. The electron-transfer hypothesis behind grounding is plausible in principle but has never been shown to work in living humans in meaningful amounts.

What is the earthing hypothesis?

The earthing hypothesis proposes that the earth's surface carries free electrons that flow into the body during direct skin contact and neutralize inflammation-driving free radicals, much as dietary antioxidants do. The chemistry is real at the edges, but the claim that enough electrons enter the body through the skin to affect inflammation has not been demonstrated.

Why might people feel better after grounding?

Grounding rarely happens in isolation. Walking barefoot outdoors bundles together sunlight, physical activity, nature exposure, and stress reduction, all of which have far better-established links to lower inflammation than skin contact with soil. Any benefit people notice is most plausibly explained by those surrounding behaviors rather than by electron transfer.

Are grounding mats worth buying?

Grounding mats, sheets, and patches marketed as anti-inflammatory devices are not supported by strong evidence and are often expensive. Barefoot time outdoors is pleasant and low-risk, but if your goal is genuinely lower inflammation, the proven levers are activity, sleep, daylight, and stress reduction, none of which require a product.
