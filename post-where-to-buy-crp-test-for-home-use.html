Where Can I Buy a CRP Test for Home Use?

There are four real places to buy one, and they sell three different things: a mail-in kit you collect at home, a lab test you order online and finish at a draw site, and a saliva kit you read yourself. Here is who sells what, at what price, and which sample each one needs.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Competitor prices and availability last verified on the sellers' own websites on the dates noted in each section, most recently November 3, 2026.

The short answer

You can buy a CRP test for home use in four places. Mail-in finger-prick kits come direct from the brands: Everlywell's Vitamin D & Inflammation Test and myLAB Box's At Home Inflammation Test were both $99 when last verified, and both return a laboratory hs-CRP value. Lab tests you order online and complete at a collection site cost less, at $69 from Labcorp OnDemand and $65 plus a $6 physician fee from Quest Health. Sensa sells the only consumer saliva CRP kit we are aware of, direct from this site, at $39 for one kit and about $26 per test in a four-pack, read with the Sensa app in 15 to 30 minutes with no lab and no needle. Marketplace and reseller listings are the fourth route and the one to approach with the most care. Sensa returns a 0 to 100 wellness score and a trend rather than an hs-CRP value, so if you need a number for a doctor, buy a lab test instead.

A disclosure before the list: this article lives on the Sensa Wellness site, and Sensa is our product. That is exactly why the sections below point you elsewhere whenever elsewhere is the better purchase. If a clinician asked you for an hs-CRP, we are not the right place to spend your money.

One note on sourcing. Every price below carries the date we last confirmed it on the seller's own site. We could not reach those sites while preparing this update, so the figures come from our most recent verified checks, dated in each section. Prices move, so open the product page before you pay.

The Four Places You Can Actually Buy One

Read the sample column before the price column. Most people shopping for a CRP test for home use are trying to avoid either a clinic visit or a needle, and those are two different goals. A mail-in kit removes the clinic but keeps the lancet. A draw-site test keeps both but costs the least. Only a saliva kit removes the needle.

Buying Direct From a Mail-In Brand

This is the route most people mean by a CRP test for home use, and two sellers dominate it. Everlywell and myLAB Box both ship a kit with a lancet, a collection card and a prepaid return mailer. You prick a fingertip, blot the card, let it dry, mail it back, and a laboratory measures hs-CRP in milligrams per liter. Both were $99 when last verified, and both bundle a vitamin D result into the same sample, a genuine extra given that vitamin D is commonly paid for on its own.

The practical differences are in the fine print. According to Everlywell's website, results are generally ready within five business days of the lab receiving your sample and are reviewed by an independent board-certified physician before release, with a report you can share with a provider. According to myLAB Box's website, results post two to five days after the lab receives the sample, a free consultation with a licensed provider is included if a result comes back positive, and FSA and HSA cards are accepted. One availability note from the same check: myLAB Box's page listed the test as temporarily unavailable in New York.

Buy here when you want a lab number without leaving the house. Plan on one to two weeks from click to result on a first order, because shipping runs both ways. We compare each against Sensa in Sensa vs Everlywell and Sensa vs myLAB Box, and our guide to at-home hs-CRP kits with lab results covers the category.

Buying a Lab Test Online and Finishing It at a Draw Site

If you are willing to drive, this is the cheapest way to get a clinical hs-CRP number. Labcorp OnDemand listed an hs-CRP test at $69 and Quest Health listed one at $65 plus a $6 physician fee when we last checked in October 2026. You buy online without a doctor's order, have blood drawn at a patient service center, and the result posts to your account. You give up kitchen-table collection and the bundled vitamin D, and you gain roughly a third off the price and usually a faster turnaround, since nothing spends days in the mail.

It is not strictly home use, and it is still often the right answer. If the number is going into a conversation with a clinician, or following up on a previously elevated result, this is where we would send you before our own product.

Marketplaces, Resellers and Listings That Have Gone Stale

This is the route where shoppers lose money, so check three things before you pay. General marketplaces carry CRP and inflammation listings from third-party sellers, and we did not verify any individual listing for this article, so treat these as questions to ask rather than a recommendation. Check the sample type, because plenty of listings say at home while meaning a lancet. Check whether a named accredited laboratory is involved at all. And check the expiration date on any reagent-based kit, which is a real risk on resold stock.

Some of the best-known names are no longer selling. On November 3, 2026, LetsGetChecked's US CRP test page displayed a message saying the product was currently unavailable, and its all-tests catalog listed no CRP or inflammation kit. A reseller, Walk-In Lab, still showed a LetsGetChecked High-Sensitivity CRP Blood Spot Test Kit at $66.75 plus a $6 physician fee on the same date, but marked it as not currently available. If an older roundup sends you there and you hit a dead end, nothing is wrong on your end. The detail is in Sensa vs LetsGetChecked.

One device-based option sits outside all of this. COR One sells a countertop reader on a membership, measuring ESR rather than CRP from a finger prick. The membership was listed at $29 per month billed annually or $39 month-to-month when we last checked on November 3, 2026, with the device included while it is active. For heavy testers the math is competitive, though the trade-offs are a needle, a device on the counter, a power outlet, Wi-Fi and a recurring charge. We lay it out in Sensa vs COR One.

What "For Home Use" Really Means on a Product Page

The phrase covers two very different products, and conflating them is the most common shopping mistake in this category. At-home collection means you take the sample and someone else measures it, which is what every mail-in kit sells. An at-home test means you take the sample and read the result yourself, which is what a saliva kit or a countertop reader does. The first gives you a clinical value after a wait; the second gives you a consumer reading in minutes. Price, speed and the kind of number you get all follow from that one distinction.
