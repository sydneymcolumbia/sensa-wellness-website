Gastritis: What an Inflamed Stomach Lining Means for Your Health

Gastritis is one of the most common reasons people feel a burning or gnawing discomfort in the upper belly. At its core, it is inflammation of the stomach lining, and understanding what drives that inflammation is the first step toward calming it.

Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining. It can be acute (sudden and short-lived) or chronic (slow-building and long-lasting). The most common cause worldwide is infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, followed by regular use of NSAID painkillers and heavy alcohol use. Most cases are manageable once the underlying cause is identified, but certain warning signs call for prompt medical care.

The stomach is a surprisingly hostile place. It produces acid strong enough to break down food and kill many swallowed microbes, and it protects itself from that acid with a thick layer of mucus and a specialized lining. Gastritis is what happens when that lining becomes inflamed. The protective balance tips, the tissue underneath is irritated, and the result can range from a vague, gnawing discomfort to sharp pain, nausea, bloating, and a feeling of fullness after only a few bites.

Gastritis is common and often misunderstood. People frequently use the word to describe any upper-belly discomfort, but it has a specific meaning: inflammation of the stomach lining itself, confirmed in many cases by looking at the tissue directly. Understanding what is actually inflaming the lining, and what is not, changes how the problem is approached.

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining (the gastric mucosa). It is described as acute when it comes on suddenly and resolves over days to weeks, and chronic when the inflammation persists over months or years, often quietly and with few obvious symptoms.

Acute Versus Chronic Gastritis

Acute gastritis appears quickly and usually settles once the trigger is removed. It might follow a bout of heavy drinking, a stretch of frequent painkiller use, a serious illness, or a stomach infection. Symptoms tend to be more noticeable: burning pain in the upper abdomen, nausea, and sometimes vomiting. Because the trigger is often recent and identifiable, acute gastritis frequently improves within a short time once that trigger stops.

Chronic gastritis is a slower, quieter process. Here the inflammation lingers for months or years, sometimes without producing dramatic symptoms at all. Over long periods, chronic inflammation can gradually change the stomach lining, thinning it or altering the cells that make up its surface. This is why chronic gastritis matters even when it does not feel urgent: the effects accumulate. The leading cause of chronic gastritis around the world is long-term H. pylori infection.

What Inflames the Stomach Lining

Gastritis is not a single disease with one cause. Several distinct triggers can inflame the gastric mucosa, and they work through different mechanisms. The table below summarizes the most common ones.

Common causes of gastritis and how each inflames the lining
CauseHow it inflames the stomach lining
H. pylori infectionThe bacterium colonizes the mucus layer and triggers a sustained immune response, keeping the lining inflamed for years if untreated.
NSAID painkillersDrugs like ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin reduce the protective prostaglandins that maintain the mucus barrier, leaving the lining exposed to acid.
Heavy alcohol useAlcohol directly irritates and erodes the mucosal surface and can loosen the protective mucus layer.

Physiological stressSerious illness, major surgery, or critical injury can reduce blood flow to the stomach lining, making it vulnerable to acid damage.

H. pylori: The Leading Cause Worldwide

Helicobacter pylori is the single most common cause of gastritis globally. This spiral-shaped bacterium is remarkably well adapted to the stomach, one of the few places in the body harsh enough to keep most microbes out. It burrows into the protective mucus layer and neutralizes acid in its immediate surroundings, allowing it to persist for years or even decades. Many people carry it without ever knowing, having acquired it in childhood.

The problem is that the immune system does not ignore H. pylori. It mounts an ongoing inflammatory response that never fully clears the infection, so the stomach lining stays inflamed. In most people this causes no obvious trouble. In a subset, however, long-term untreated infection is associated with more serious problems over time, including peptic ulcers and, less commonly, changes in the stomach lining that raise the risk of stomach cancer. This is why H. pylori is taken seriously even when symptoms are mild.

Testing for and treating H. pylori is firmly a conversation to have with a healthcare provider. There are several ways to test for it, and treatment typically involves a specific combination of antibiotics and acid-reducing medication chosen by a clinician. Self-treatment is not appropriate here, both because accurate diagnosis matters and because incomplete treatment can contribute to antibiotic resistance.

NSAID and Alcohol-Related Gastritis

Regular use of NSAID painkillers is one of the most common non-infectious causes of gastritis. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, including ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin, work in part by blocking enzymes that produce prostaglandins. Some of those same prostaglandins maintain the stomach's protective mucus and bicarbonate barrier. When NSAIDs suppress them, the lining loses some of its natural defense against acid, and inflammation or even erosion can follow. The risk rises with higher doses, longer use, and combining NSAIDs with other irritants. If you rely on these medications regularly, it is worth discussing the safest approach with a clinician rather than simply stopping on your own.

Heavy alcohol use inflames the lining more directly. Alcohol is an irritant to the gastric mucosa and can strip away part of the protective mucus layer, exposing the tissue underneath. Occasional moderate drinking is unlikely to cause lasting gastritis in most people, but frequent or heavy drinking is a well-recognized trigger. Because alcohol also drives inflammation throughout the body, cutting back tends to help the stomach and general inflammatory load at the same time.

Autoimmune Gastritis and Vitamin B12

Autoimmune gastritis is a distinct, less common form in which the immune system attacks the stomach's own cells. Specifically, it targets the parietal cells that produce stomach acid and a substance called intrinsic factor. Intrinsic factor is essential for absorbing vitamin B12 from food. When these cells are damaged over time, two things happen: the stomach makes less acid, and B12 absorption falls.

Because B12 is needed to make healthy red blood cells and support the nervous system, autoimmune gastritis can eventually lead to a specific type of anemia and, if unaddressed, nerve-related symptoms. The inflammation itself may cause few stomach symptoms, which is part of why this form can go unrecognized for a long time. Diagnosis and management, including whether B12 supplementation is needed and in what form, are handled by a healthcare provider.

Gastritis Is Not the Same as Indigestion or GERD

These terms are often used interchangeably, but they describe different things. Indigestion, or dyspepsia, is a general term for upper-belly discomfort, fullness, or burning after eating. It is a description of symptoms, not a diagnosis, and it can have many causes, including gastritis but also many others.

Gastroesophageal reflux disease, or GERD, is a separate condition involving stomach contents flowing back up into the esophagus, typically producing heartburn and regurgitation. GERD is centered on the esophagus and the valve between it and the stomach, whereas gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining itself. The two can coexist and can feel similar, which is why they are easy to confuse. Lifestyle Factors Linked to a Calmer Stomach Lining

While the underlying cause of gastritis often needs medical attention, especially with H. pylori or autoimmune disease, research consistently associates several everyday habits with a less irritated stomach lining. These are supportive measures, not replacements for diagnosis and treatment.

  • Steady meal patterns. Regular, moderate meals tend to be gentler on the stomach than long fasts followed by large, heavy meals. Many people find that eating smaller amounts more often reduces discomfort.
  • Moderating alcohol. Reducing frequency and quantity removes one of the most direct chemical irritants to the gastric lining.
  • Not smoking. Smoking is associated with impaired healing of the stomach lining and a higher risk of ulcers, so cessation supports recovery.
  • Managing stress. Psychological stress does not cause gastritis on its own, but it can worsen symptoms and influence acid-related discomfort. Stress management techniques can make the digestive experience more comfortable.
  • Reviewing painkiller use. If you take NSAIDs frequently, talking with a clinician about alternatives or protective strategies can reduce ongoing irritation. For a broader look at soothing the stomach, see our guide to reducing inflammation in the stomach.

Red Flags That Need Prompt Medical Care

Most gastritis is uncomfortable rather than dangerous, but certain symptoms suggest bleeding or a more serious problem and should not be brushed off. Seek prompt medical attention if you experience any of the following:

  • Black, tarry stools, which can indicate bleeding in the upper digestive tract.
  • Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds.
  • Unintentional weight loss that you cannot explain.
  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain, particularly if it is sudden or worsening.
  • Difficulty swallowing or persistent vomiting that prevents you from keeping food down.

These signs warrant evaluation because they can point to ulcers, significant bleeding, or other conditions that need timely treatment. When in doubt, it is always reasonable to check in with a healthcare provider.

Tracking Inflammation as Part of the Bigger Picture

Gastritis is a local problem, but it sits within your body's wider inflammatory landscape, and the habits that calm the stomach lining, moderating alcohol, not smoking, managing stress, and eating well, tend to lower systemic inflammation too. That makes it useful to have a general sense of your overall inflammatory load over time.

C-reactive protein, or CRP, is a widely used marker of general inflammation in the body. It is important to be clear about what it can and cannot do: CRP is not a test for gastritis and cannot diagnose it. Gastritis is diagnosed by a clinician, often by examining the stomach lining directly or testing for H. pylori. What CRP can offer is a broad wellness signal about your general inflammatory trend. Because CRP is a wellness marker rather than a diagnosis, any persistent elevation, and certainly any of the red flags above, should be discussed with a healthcare provider.

Gastritis is common, usually treatable, and rarely a mystery once the cause is identified. The most useful mindset is a practical one: notice the pattern, find the trigger, take the warning signs seriously, and support your stomach with habits that keep inflammation low across the board.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is gastritis?

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining, also called the gastric mucosa. It can be acute, coming on suddenly and resolving over days to weeks, or chronic, persisting for months or years, often quietly. Symptoms may include burning or gnawing upper-belly discomfort, nausea, bloating, and feeling full quickly, though some people have no obvious symptoms at all.

What is the most common cause of gastritis?

Worldwide, the leading cause is infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, which colonizes the stomach lining and triggers long-lasting inflammation. Other common causes include regular use of NSAID painkillers such as ibuprofen and aspirin, heavy alcohol use, autoimmune activity, and physiological stress from serious illness. Testing and treatment for H. pylori is a conversation to have with a healthcare provider.

What is the difference between gastritis and GERD?

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining itself, while GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease) involves stomach contents flowing back up into the esophagus, typically causing heartburn and regurgitation. Indigestion, or dyspepsia, is a general term for upper-belly discomfort and is a description of symptoms rather than a diagnosis. The conditions can feel similar and can coexist.

When should gastritis symptoms be checked by a doctor?

Seek prompt medical care if you have black or tarry stools, vomiting of blood or coffee-ground-like material, unintentional weight loss, severe or persistent abdominal pain, or trouble swallowing. These can signal bleeding or a more serious problem. Any persistent stomach symptoms are worth discussing with a healthcare provider so the underlying cause can be identified.
