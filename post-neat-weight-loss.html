NEAT: The Calories You Burn Without Exercising

The single biggest swing in your daily calorie burn is not your workout. It is everything you do the rest of the day: standing, walking, fidgeting, and moving. That is NEAT, and most people leave it on the table.

Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

NEAT stands for non-exercise activity thermogenesis: the calories you burn from all the movement that is not formal exercise, like standing, walking, fidgeting, and doing chores. It can vary by hundreds of calories a day between people, it quietly falls when you diet, and building it back up is often a bigger lever for weight loss than adding another workout.

Most people think of daily calorie burn as two things: their resting metabolism and their workout. But there is a third bucket that is larger and far more flexible than either, and almost nobody plans around it. It is the movement that happens outside the gym, and researchers call it NEAT.

Understanding NEAT changes how you think about weight loss. It explains why two people who eat the same and exercise the same can have very different results, why the scale stalls even when your training does not, and why a genuinely active day can beat a sedentary day with a workout tacked on.

NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) is the energy you expend on everything that is not sleeping, eating, or formal exercise: walking to the mailbox, cooking, taking the stairs, standing at your desk, and even fidgeting.

Your total daily energy expenditure has four components. Your basal metabolic rate keeps the lights on and is largely fixed. The thermic effect of food is the small cost of digestion. Formal exercise is what you deliberately schedule. And NEAT is everything else you do while awake. For someone with a desk job, formal exercise might account for a modest slice of the day, while NEAT can be the single most variable piece, swinging widely depending on how the rest of the hours are spent.

How Much Can NEAT Really Vary?

The wide range in NEAT between people is what makes it so important. Research on non-exercise activity has repeatedly found that the day-to-day movement of otherwise similar individuals can differ by hundreds of calories, and this gap helps explain why some people seem to resist weight gain while others do not. The difference is not willpower at the dinner table. It is how much they move without thinking about it.

This matters because NEAT is highly individual and highly changeable. Two people of the same size can have very different totals simply because one paces during phone calls, takes the stairs, and cannot sit still, while the other is efficient and still. Neither is doing anything you would recognize as a workout. Over a week, those quiet differences add up to far more energy than most single exercise sessions.

Why NEAT Quietly Drops When You Diet

One of the most frustrating features of weight loss is the plateau, and NEAT is a big part of the explanation. When you cut calories, your body defends its energy stores in several ways, and one of the most underappreciated is that it makes you move less without your permission. This is sometimes described as the body economizing on movement.

You do not decide to fidget less or take fewer steps. It happens below the level of conscious choice. You sit a little sooner, you take the elevator without noticing, your spontaneous movement dials down, and your total burn quietly falls even though your workouts look identical on paper. This adaptive drop in NEAT can erase a meaningful part of the deficit you worked to create, which is a major reason progress stalls even when nothing about your diet or training has visibly changed. Recognizing this lets you counter it deliberately, by protecting your daily steps and movement when you are eating less rather than letting them slide.

NEAT Versus an Hour at the Gym

It is tempting to treat a workout as the part of the day that counts and the rest as neutral. The energy math argues otherwise. A single gym session burns a set number of calories and then it is over. NEAT, by contrast, is spread across every waking hour, so a person who moves consistently all day can out-burn a person who trains hard for one hour and then sits for the rest of it.

This is not an argument against exercise. Regular training has benefits that go far beyond calorie burn, from cardiovascular fitness to muscle preservation, and it belongs in any healthy routine. The point is that a workout does not cancel out a sedentary day. If you crush a session and then spend the remaining fifteen waking hours seated, your total movement can still land below that of someone who never formally exercised but stayed active throughout the day. The most effective approach is to do both: train deliberately and keep your baseline movement high. Desk Jobs and Practical Ways to Rebuild NEAT

The modern desk job is essentially a NEAT-suppression machine. Long stretches of uninterrupted sitting are the default, and the costs go beyond calories: prolonged sitting is independently linked to metabolic and inflammatory changes, which we cover in detail in our article on the sedentary lifestyle and chronic inflammation. The good news is that NEAT responds quickly to small, repeatable changes in how you structure your day.

You do not need a standing desk or a gym membership to move the needle. You need friction removed from movement and a few habits stacked onto things you already do. Practical NEAT builders include:

  • Walking meetings and pacing on calls. Any call that does not require a screen is an opportunity to be on your feet and moving instead of seated.
  • Take the stairs by default. Treat the elevator as the exception rather than the rule for a few flights.
  • Stand and move on a timer. A brief stand-and-stretch break every half hour to hour breaks up the longest sitting stretches, which is where much of the harm concentrates.
  • Walk after meals. Even a short walk after eating adds movement at a time when it is easy to slump into a chair.
  • Park farther, get off a stop early, carry things in more trips. These are the classic inefficiencies that add movement without adding time you have to schedule.
  • Do your own chores actively. Cooking, tidying, and yard work are all NEAT, and doing them at a slightly brisker pace counts for more.

None of these feels like exercise, which is exactly the point. They slot into the day you already have, and because they are small, they are sustainable in a way that heroic workout plans often are not.

The Anti-Inflammatory Angle of Breaking Up Sitting

NEAT is usually framed as a weight-loss tool, but its value extends to inflammation. The problem with a sedentary day is not only the low calorie burn; it is the sheer length of the uninterrupted sitting. Breaking up that sitting time with frequent light movement is associated with better metabolic markers than sitting for the same total hours in one long block.

This is why building NEAT and lowering chronic inflammation tend to move together. When you stand, walk, and interrupt long sedentary stretches throughout the day, you are engaging muscles, improving glucose handling, and avoiding the metabolic conditions that favor a low-grade inflammatory state. Weight loss itself also tends to lower inflammation, a relationship we explore in how inflammation can sabotage weight loss. Raising NEAT is one of the few habits that quietly serves both goals at once.

Making NEAT Automatic Through Environment Design

The reason NEAT is easy to lose and hard to force is that it is driven by habit and environment, not motivation. Willpower is a poor long-term strategy for something you are supposed to do all day, every day. A better approach is to design your surroundings so that movement is the path of least resistance.

That means putting the printer across the room, keeping walking shoes by the door, scheduling recurring stand-up reminders, choosing a lunch spot that requires a walk, and setting up your space so sitting for hours takes effort rather than happening by default. When movement is the easy option, you do not have to decide to do it. The goal is to make an active day something that happens to you because of how your environment is arranged, rather than something you have to summon the discipline to pull off.

Tracking Movement, Weight, and Inflammation Together

Because NEAT is invisible by nature, it helps to make it visible. A simple step count or activity tracker turns a vague sense of how much you moved into a number you can watch, and watching it is often enough to keep it from silently drifting down during a diet. Pairing that with regular weigh-ins gives you the two halves of the energy-balance picture that actually respond to your habits.

Inflammation is the third signal worth watching, because the same behaviors that raise NEAT and support weight loss also tend to lower chronic inflammation. C-reactive protein, or CRP, is a practical marker of that low-grade inflammatory state, and you can read more about what it reflects in our guide to understanding CRP. Sensa is a general wellness tool that lets you check CRP at home, without a needle or a clinic visit, so an inflammation reading becomes a routine data point rather than a rare event. Watching your CRP trend hold steady or drift downward as you build more movement into your days is a tangible sign that the small, unglamorous habits are adding up. CRP is a general wellness marker rather than a diagnosis, so persistent elevations are worth discussing with a healthcare provider.

NEAT will never feel dramatic. It is the opposite of a bold new program. But for most people, the calories burned quietly across an active day represent the largest and most controllable lever they have, and learning to protect and grow it may do more for long-term weight management than any single workout.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does NEAT stand for?

NEAT stands for non-exercise activity thermogenesis. It is the energy you burn from all the movement that is not sleeping, eating, or formal exercise, including standing, walking, doing chores, taking the stairs, and fidgeting. For many people with desk jobs, NEAT is the largest and most variable part of daily calorie burn outside of resting metabolism.

Why does weight loss stall even when I keep exercising?

One major reason is that NEAT quietly falls when you diet. When you cut calories, your body tends to economize on movement without your conscious input: you fidget less, take fewer spontaneous steps, and sit down sooner. This adaptive drop can erase part of the calorie deficit you created, so the scale stalls even though your workouts have not changed. Protecting your daily steps and movement while eating less helps counter it.

Can NEAT burn more calories than a workout?

It often can, because NEAT is spread across every waking hour while a workout is a single block of time. Someone who stays active all day, walking, standing, and moving, can out-burn someone who trains hard for one hour and then sits for the rest of it. This is not a reason to skip exercise, which has benefits beyond calories, but a reason to keep your baseline daily movement high as well.

How can I increase my NEAT with a desk job?

Focus on small, repeatable habits that remove friction from movement: take walking meetings and pace during calls, use the stairs by default, stand and move on a timer to break up long sitting stretches, walk after meals, and design your environment so movement is the easy option. None of these feels like exercise, which makes them easier to sustain day after day.
