Does the DASH Diet Reduce Inflammation? What the Evidence Shows

Designed to lower blood pressure, the DASH diet turned out to have a second benefit researchers did not fully anticipate: it also moves inflammatory markers in the right direction, including C-reactive protein.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes, with meaningful evidence. A 2017 meta-analysis of randomized trials found that the DASH diet significantly lowered high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) compared to a typical Western diet, with an average reduction of about 1 mg/L. A 2023 reanalysis of the original DASH-Sodium trial confirmed the effect, showing hs-CRP fell progressively over 12 weeks on the DASH diet and was nearly 20 percent lower than the control group by the end. The anti-inflammatory effect is likely a consequence of the diet's core design: high in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy, and low in saturated fat and sodium, it delivers a bundle of nutrients that work through several anti-inflammatory pathways at once.

The DASH diet has one of the most well-documented track records in nutrition research. When researchers set out in the mid-1990s to find a dietary pattern that could lower blood pressure without drugs, they landed on a combination that turned out to be broadly health-promoting in ways that went beyond blood pressure. Its effects on inflammation are a relatively recent focus of investigation, but the evidence is consistent: following the DASH pattern tends to push hs-CRP downward, and the magnitude of the effect is clinically meaningful.

DASH diet stands for Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension. It emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, low-fat dairy, poultry, fish, beans, and nuts while limiting red and processed meats, full-fat dairy, sodium, sugary beverages, and sweets.

What the DASH Diet Actually Looks Like

The DASH diet is not a branded program or a commercial plan, but a specific eating pattern developed and tested in clinical research. The original trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1997 by Appel and colleagues (Appel et al., 1997), enrolled 459 adults and compared three dietary patterns over eight weeks: a typical American control diet, a fruits-and-vegetables diet, and a combination diet that became what we now call DASH. The combination diet reduced systolic blood pressure by 5.5 mm Hg more than the control diet on average, and 11.4 mm Hg more in the subset of participants who already had hypertension. This was striking enough to influence dietary guidelines worldwide.

What distinguishes DASH from generic "healthy eating" advice is that it has specific nutrient and food group targets. On a 2,000-calorie plan, the standard guidance calls for roughly 6 to 8 servings of whole grains per day, 4 to 5 servings of vegetables, 4 to 5 servings of fruit, 2 to 3 servings of low-fat dairy, 6 or fewer ounces of lean meats or fish, 4 to 5 servings of nuts, seeds, or legumes per week, and limited sodium (ideally under 1,500 mg per day, though 2,300 mg is the more commonly used target). Sweets and added sugars are kept to 5 or fewer servings per week. These targets create a diet that is naturally high in potassium, magnesium, calcium, and fiber, and low in saturated fat, sodium, and refined carbohydrates.

This nutrient profile overlaps substantially with the Mediterranean diet, though DASH is more explicit about limiting sodium and includes more low-fat dairy. Both are associated with reduced cardiovascular disease risk and lower inflammatory markers, and they share a foundation in plant foods and lean protein. The practical difference for most people is that DASH was developed specifically in a hypertension context and tends to be more prescriptive about sodium, while the Mediterranean diet is more permissive about healthy fats like olive oil and traditionally includes moderate wine.

How the DASH Diet Targets Inflammation

The anti-inflammatory effect of the DASH diet is not driven by one single nutrient, but by a combination of them working simultaneously. This is an important distinction from the literature on individual supplements. When researchers try to lower CRP with a single compound, the effect is often modest and variable. When they substitute an entire dietary pattern, the effect tends to be more consistent, because multiple anti-inflammatory pathways are addressed at once.

Fiber is one of the most direct levers. The DASH diet's emphasis on vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and legumes substantially raises fiber intake. Dietary fiber feeds the gut microbiome, which responds by producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate. Butyrate acts directly on immune cells lining the gut and inhibits the activation of NF-kappa-B, the master transcription factor that drives the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Higher fiber intake is consistently associated with lower CRP in observational data, and the DASH diet delivers fiber in quantities that most people eating a typical Western diet do not come close to reaching.

The diet is also rich in polyphenols, plant compounds with established antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Berries, apples, grapes, leafy greens, tomatoes, legumes, and whole grains all contribute polyphenols such as flavonoids and phenolic acids. These compounds can inhibit pro-inflammatory enzymes and signaling molecules including cyclooxygenase, NF-kappa-B, and NLRP3 inflammasome pathways. Because DASH recommends several daily servings of fruits and vegetables, the cumulative polyphenol load is substantially higher than what a Western diet typically delivers.

Magnesium is another mechanism worth highlighting. The DASH diet is a good source of magnesium, delivered through dark leafy greens, nuts, seeds, and whole grains. Magnesium deficiency is widespread in populations eating refined-food diets and has been linked to elevated inflammatory signaling. Adequate magnesium helps regulate the NLRP3 inflammasome and suppresses the production of IL-6 and TNF-alpha. Correcting a subclinical shortfall through the diet may therefore contribute meaningfully to the CRP reductions observed in DASH trials, particularly in participants who start with low dietary magnesium.

Finally, what the DASH diet removes matters as much as what it adds. By limiting saturated fat, processed meats, and added sugars, the diet cuts three of the most reliably pro-inflammatory elements of modern eating. Saturated fat activates toll-like receptor 4 on immune cells, triggering an inflammatory response. Processed meats are associated with higher IL-6 and CRP in epidemiological studies. High added sugar intake drives systemic inflammation by increasing advanced glycation end products, stimulating lipopolysaccharide production in the gut, and elevating blood glucose in ways that activate inflammatory cascades. Removing these inputs while adding fiber, polyphenols, and micronutrients creates a dietary environment where chronic inflammatory tone can meaningfully decrease.

What the Clinical Evidence Shows

The clearest summary of the trial evidence comes from a 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Clinical Nutrition. Soltani, Chitsazi, and Salehi-Abargouei searched PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Google Scholar through December 2016, identified seven eligible randomized trials, and pooled six studies with 451 participants that measured hs-CRP as the primary inflammatory biomarker (Soltani et al., 2017). The result was a statistically significant reduction in hs-CRP on the DASH diet compared to a usual Western diet (mean difference: -1.01 mg/L, 95% CI: -1.64 to -0.38). The authors noted that the reduction was greater in trials lasting eight weeks or longer, suggesting that the effect accumulates over time rather than appearing immediately. Heterogeneity across studies was high, which the authors attributed to differences in study duration, baseline hs-CRP, and comparator diets. When DASH was compared specifically against other healthy diets (rather than a typical Western diet), the difference disappeared, implying that any reasonably high-quality diet can lower CRP and that DASH's advantage lies in its contrast with the standard Western pattern.

A 2023 reanalysis of the DASH-Sodium trial added important detail about how quickly the effect develops. Belanger and colleagues measured hs-CRP, high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I, and NT-proBNP in stored serum samples from the original trial of 412 adults with elevated blood pressure, taken at baseline and at four, eight, and twelve weeks after randomization (Belanger et al., 2023). The DASH diet produced a progressive reduction in hs-CRP over the 12-week period, and the difference from the control diet reached statistical significance at week 12, with the DASH group showing hs-CRP levels approximately 20 percent lower (19.97%, 95% CI: -31.94% to -5.89%). A similar progressive pattern emerged for high-sensitivity cardiac troponin I. The word "progressive" matters here: the reduction did not occur all at once in the first week, but accumulated across the duration of the trial. This is consistent with the meta-analysis finding that longer trials show stronger effects.

Broader systematic reviews of dietary patterns confirm the direction of the DASH finding. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in Nutrition Journal by Hart and colleagues examined 69 studies covering multiple dietary pattern scores and found that adherence to healthy, Mediterranean, and anti-inflammatory diet scores was consistently associated with lower inflammatory status across the population (Hart et al., 2021). DASH-style adherence was among the patterns most reliably linked to lower CRP. While most of the included studies were cross-sectional rather than interventional, the consistency of the association across different populations and measurement methods gives it credibility beyond any single trial.

How Long Does It Take to See a Change in CRP?

Based on the DASH-Sodium trial reanalysis, meaningful changes in hs-CRP can take 8 to 12 weeks to reach statistical significance. The progressive nature of the effect suggests that the diet needs time to shift the microbiome, reduce visceral fat if weight loss occurs, and normalize the chronic background of low-grade inflammation driven by the prior dietary pattern. This has practical implications for anyone who wants to track their inflammation levels. A single CRP test taken two weeks after changing eating habits is unlikely to capture the full effect. Measuring at baseline, then again at 8 and 12 weeks, gives a more informative picture of trajectory.

Weight loss, if it occurs alongside the dietary change, amplifies the effect. The ENCORE study compared DASH alone against DASH plus a structured weight management program in 144 adults with above-normal blood pressure (Blumenthal et al., 2010). The combined intervention produced greater reductions in blood pressure and cardiovascular biomarkers than DASH alone. Because visceral adipose tissue is a major source of pro-inflammatory adipokines including leptin and resistin, reducing body fat independently lowers the inflammatory set point. DASH can facilitate weight loss because it is satiating, nutrient-dense, and structurally incompatible with the high-calorie-density foods that drive weight gain in the Western diet. When weight comes down, CRP tends to follow.

Putting DASH into Practice

One of the practical advantages of DASH is that it does not require eliminating any entire food group. Unlike elimination diets or restrictive frameworks, DASH works by shifting proportions: more plants, more fiber, more low-fat dairy, and less sodium, saturated fat, and sugar. For most people, the most impactful changes are adding a serving or two of vegetables per day, switching from refined to whole grains, and replacing processed snacks with nuts, legumes, or fruit. These are smaller behavioral changes than dropping entire macronutrient categories, which makes DASH easier to sustain long-term.

Sodium reduction is the most counter-cultural part of DASH for people eating a typical Western diet. Ultra-processed foods, restaurant meals, and packaged snacks are primary sources of sodium in most modern diets, and many people consuming them have no accurate sense of how much sodium they are taking in. Switching toward whole and minimally processed foods, cooking more at home, and using herbs and spices in place of salt are the most effective practical steps for bringing sodium down. The inflammation benefit likely reflects the full dietary pattern rather than sodium reduction alone, but sodium is a component of the design that matters for the cardiovascular outcomes the diet was originally built to address.

DASH also works well as a framework for people who have been told their CRP is elevated and want to make lifestyle changes before discussing medication options with their healthcare provider. Because the diet addresses multiple inflammatory mechanisms simultaneously and has a substantial evidence base, it is one of the more credible dietary approaches for someone specifically trying to lower hs-CRP. Combined with regular moderate exercise, adequate sleep, and stress management, it represents the kind of multi-pronged lifestyle approach that tends to produce the most durable changes in inflammatory markers over time. A home CRP test at baseline and again after 10 to 12 weeks of consistent adherence can help you see whether the pattern is working, and those numbers are always worth sharing with your healthcare team.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the DASH diet reduce inflammation?

Yes. A 2017 meta-analysis of randomized trials found that the DASH diet significantly reduced high-sensitivity CRP by an average of about 1 mg/L compared to a typical Western diet. A 2023 reanalysis of the DASH-Sodium trial confirmed the effect, showing hs-CRP fell progressively over 12 weeks and was nearly 20 percent lower than the control group by week 12.

How long does it take for the DASH diet to lower CRP?

Based on trial data, meaningful reductions in hs-CRP tend to emerge over 8 to 12 weeks of consistent adherence. The effect is progressive rather than immediate, which means checking CRP too early after starting the diet may underestimate the benefit. Tracking CRP at baseline and again after about 10 to 12 weeks provides a more informative picture.

Why does the DASH diet lower inflammation?

Several mechanisms work together. The diet is rich in dietary fiber, which feeds anti-inflammatory gut bacteria. It delivers a high load of polyphenols from fruits and vegetables, which inhibit inflammatory signaling pathways. It is also a good source of magnesium, a mineral important for regulating inflammatory responses. At the same time, DASH limits saturated fat, processed meats, and added sugars, all of which are established drivers of systemic inflammation.

Is the DASH diet better than the Mediterranean diet for inflammation?

When researchers compared the DASH diet against other healthy dietary patterns (rather than the typical Western diet), the CRP-lowering difference was no longer significant. Both DASH and Mediterranean diets improve inflammatory markers substantially compared to a standard Western diet, and they share most of their core design elements. The main practical differences are that DASH is more explicit about limiting sodium and includes more low-fat dairy, while Mediterranean allows more olive oil and traditionally includes moderate wine.
