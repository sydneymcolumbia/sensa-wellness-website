Does L-Theanine Reduce Inflammation? What the Evidence Shows

L-theanine is the amino acid in green tea most people know for producing calm, focused alertness. Whether that same compound meaningfully quiets inflammation is a more nuanced question, and the honest answer sits at the intersection of stress biology and immune signaling.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Partly, and mostly in indirect ways. L-theanine, the signature amino acid in green tea, has a well-documented ability to lower the body's stress response in human trials, and because chronic psychological stress is a known driver of inflammation, this is probably its most relevant route to a calmer inflammatory state. Direct evidence that L-theanine lowers C-reactive protein in healthy people is limited, and the strongest human inflammation data come from studies of a cystine and theanine combination rather than theanine alone. Animal and cell studies show L-theanine can suppress inflammatory signaling and support the body's antioxidant defenses, but large human trials measuring CRP as a primary outcome have not been done. The realistic takeaway is that L-theanine is a low-risk, calming compound with plausible anti-inflammatory effects, not a proven CRP-lowering intervention.

Walk into any supplement aisle or scan the ingredient list of a nootropic drink and you will find L-theanine almost everywhere. It is marketed for focus, for taking the edge off caffeine, for sleep, and increasingly for stress. What is far less commonly discussed is whether L-theanine does anything for inflammation, the low-grade immune activation that sits beneath so many chronic health concerns. The question matters because the pathways that govern stress and the pathways that govern inflammation are deeply intertwined, and L-theanine acts squarely on the stress side of that relationship.

L-theanine (chemical name N-ethyl-L-glutamine) is a non-protein amino acid found almost exclusively in tea leaves and a few mushroom species. It is structurally similar to glutamate and glutamine, crosses the blood-brain barrier, and is best known for promoting a state of relaxed alertness without sedation.

Why L-Theanine Is Not a Typical Anti-Inflammatory

Most anti-inflammatory compounds are studied for how they act directly on immune cells, but L-theanine earned its reputation in the brain. After you drink tea, L-theanine is absorbed in the small intestine and travels to the brain, where it influences several neurotransmitter systems. It is thought to increase levels of GABA, the body's main calming neurotransmitter, along with dopamine and serotonin, while also promoting alpha brain wave activity associated with a relaxed but wakeful state. This is why L-theanine is paired with caffeine in so many products: it appears to smooth out the jittery edge of stimulation while preserving the alertness.

This neurological starting point is exactly why its relationship to inflammation is indirect rather than obvious. L-theanine was not discovered as an immune modulator and then applied to the brain. It was studied as a calming agent, and only later did researchers begin asking whether its effects on stress physiology and oxidative balance might ripple outward to the immune system. According to a 2023 review in the journal Molecules titled "L-Theanine and Immunity," the immunomodulatory effects of L-theanine have historically received far less attention than its sedative and calming activities, even though a growing body of cell and animal work suggests it plays a regulatory role in inflammation, nerve tissue, and the gut (Chen et al., 2023).

The Stress Connection: L-Theanine's Most Established Human Effect

The single best-supported thing L-theanine does in humans is blunt the stress response, and this is the most plausible way it touches inflammation. Chronic psychological stress raises cortisol and sympathetic nervous system activity, and sustained stress is consistently associated with higher levels of inflammatory markers over time. Anything that reliably lowers the body's stress load therefore has a credible, if indirect, path to a lower inflammatory burden. This is the mechanism that connects a calming amino acid to a topic like CRP.

A randomized controlled trial provides some of the clearest human evidence for the stress effect. In a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study published in Nutrients, researchers gave 30 healthy adults either 200 milligrams of L-theanine per day or a placebo for four weeks. Scores on validated measures of depression, anxiety, and sleep quality all decreased significantly during the L-theanine phase compared with placebo, with particular improvements in how quickly people fell asleep and how disrupted their sleep was (Hidese et al., 2019). Better sleep and lower anxiety are both themselves associated with lower inflammatory activity, which is why this trial matters to the inflammation question even though it did not measure CRP.

An earlier study captured the physiological side of the stress response directly. In a double-blind laboratory experiment published in Biological Psychology, participants performed a stressful mental arithmetic task after taking L-theanine or placebo. L-theanine intake reduced the heart rate response and the salivary immunoglobulin A response to the acute stressor, and heart rate variability analysis suggested the effect came from dampening sympathetic nervous system activation (Kimura et al., 2007). In plain terms, L-theanine appeared to keep the body's fight-or-flight machinery from ramping up as hard, which is relevant because chronic sympathetic activation is one of the links between stress and inflammatory signaling.

Direct Evidence on Inflammation and CRP

The most direct human inflammation data on theanine come from studies of a combined cystine and theanine supplement, not theanine on its own. This distinction is important and often glossed over in marketing. Cystine supplies the raw material for glutathione, the body's master antioxidant, and theanine appears to support that same antioxidant system. Several Japanese research groups have tested this combination in situations that reliably trigger inflammation, such as intense endurance training and major surgery.

In athletes, the combination blunted the inflammatory spike that normally follows hard training. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, long-distance runners took either cystine and theanine or a placebo for ten days before a period of intense training. The placebo group showed a significant rise in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and neutrophil counts after the training, along with a tendency toward falling lymphocytes, while the cystine and theanine group did not show these changes (Murakami et al., 2009). This is one of the few human datasets in which a theanine-containing supplement was linked to a blunted CRP response, and it is worth noting the effect was measured against a physical stressor rather than in resting, healthy conditions.

A surgical trial points in the same direction with a clinical endpoint. In a randomized, double-blind, controlled trial published in Nutrients, patients undergoing esophagectomy, a major operation that provokes a strong inflammatory response, received either perioperative cystine and theanine or placebo. White blood cell counts, neutrophil counts, and C-reactive protein were significantly lower in the cystine and theanine group at several points after surgery, and those patients also walked farther and reported better recovery quality (Okamoto et al., 2022). Again, the signal is that theanine, delivered alongside cystine, helped keep the inflammatory response in check when the body was under acute stress.

The honest reading of this evidence is that it is suggestive but not conclusive for L-theanine alone. The combination trials are genuinely informative, but they cannot tell us how much of the benefit came from theanine versus cystine. No large randomized trial has tested L-theanine by itself with CRP as the main outcome in generally healthy people. That is a meaningfully different evidence picture from compounds like omega-3 fatty acids, where the resting-state CRP literature is far more developed. Anyone reading claims that L-theanine lowers inflammation should hold that gap in mind.

How L-Theanine Might Calm Inflammation at the Cellular Level

Animal and cell research offers a coherent mechanistic story, even though it cannot substitute for human trials. The two most consistent threads are antioxidant support and the suppression of pro-inflammatory signaling. Oxidative stress and inflammation are tightly coupled: reactive oxygen species activate inflammatory pathways, and inflammation in turn generates more oxidative stress, so a compound that strengthens antioxidant defenses can indirectly lower inflammatory tone.

L-theanine appears to support glutathione, the body's central antioxidant. This is the same mechanism that gives the cystine and theanine combination its rationale. By helping maintain glutathione levels under stress, L-theanine may limit the oxidative damage that would otherwise feed inflammation. In animal models, this has translated into measurable reductions in classic inflammatory cytokines. In one mouse study of heat stress, L-theanine supplementation countered the rise in TNF-alpha, IL-6, and IL-1beta and preserved antioxidant enzyme activity, with the authors attributing the effect in part to modulation of the p38 MAPK signaling pathway and downregulation of NF-kB signaling, the master switch for inflammatory gene expression.

The gut is another plausible site of action. A substantial fraction of the body's immune activity resides in and around the intestinal tract, and animal work suggests L-theanine can influence the gut microbiome, strengthen the intestinal barrier, and shift immune cell behavior toward a less inflammatory profile. Because a compromised gut barrier is one recognized source of the bacterial fragments that drive low-grade systemic inflammation, effects at this level could matter for overall inflammatory tone. These findings are promising, but they remain largely confined to rodent models and have not been confirmed with inflammatory marker measurements in humans.

Food Sources and Supplementation

Tea is effectively the only meaningful dietary source of L-theanine. Green tea, black tea, white tea, and matcha all contain it, with matcha generally providing more because the whole leaf is consumed. The actual amount per cup is modest and varies widely with the type of tea, steeping time, and water temperature, so a single cup delivers only a fraction of the amounts used in clinical research. For people who simply enjoy tea, L-theanine is one of several compounds, alongside polyphenols like EGCG, that make tea a reasonable part of an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern.

Supplemental doses in human studies have generally ranged from 100 to 400 milligrams per day. The stress and sleep trial described above used 200 milligrams daily, which sits in the middle of that range and is a common commercial dose. L-theanine is frequently combined with caffeine in ratios intended to preserve alertness while reducing jitteriness. There is no established dose specifically for anti-inflammatory purposes, because the human trials have not been designed around that outcome.

L-theanine has a strong safety profile at the doses typically studied. It is generally well tolerated, with few reported side effects, and it does not appear to cause the drowsiness associated with many calming agents. Mild headaches have occasionally been reported. As with any supplement, people who are pregnant, nursing, taking medications, or managing a health condition should talk to a healthcare provider before starting, particularly because L-theanine can interact with the effects of stimulants and blood pressure medications.

Where L-Theanine Fits in the Bigger Picture

The most defensible way to think about L-theanine is as a tool for managing stress, with any anti-inflammatory benefit flowing from that. If chronic stress, poor sleep, and sympathetic overactivation are contributing to your inflammatory load, a compound that reliably eases those states has a credible route to helping. That is a meaningfully different claim from saying L-theanine directly suppresses CRP the way a targeted anti-inflammatory drug might. The evidence supports the former far more strongly than the latter.

It also fits naturally into patterns that are themselves anti-inflammatory. People who drink tea regularly, sleep well, and manage stress tend to have lower inflammatory markers for a whole cluster of reasons, and separating the specific contribution of L-theanine from that broader picture is difficult. This is a recurring theme with gentle, food-derived compounds: they are best understood as supporting pieces of an overall lifestyle rather than standalone fixes.

For anyone tracking their inflammatory status, L-theanine is a reasonable low-risk addition, not a lever that will move CRP on its own. The dominant drivers of chronic inflammation remain body composition, physical activity, sleep, diet quality, smoking, and chronic stress. L-theanine touches the stress and sleep corners of that list, which is genuinely useful, but it is one modest input among many. Because CRP is a general wellness marker and not a diagnostic test, any persistent elevation is worth discussing with a healthcare provider regardless of which supplements you are taking.
