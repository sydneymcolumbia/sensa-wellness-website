How to Test Inflammation Levels at Home: 4 Options Compared

You do not need a clinic visit to learn something about your inflammation. There are four practical routes, from a doctor-ordered blood test to a saliva kit you read in your kitchen. Here is what each one measures, what it costs, how long it takes, and how to build a routine that actually tells you something.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

There are four ways to test inflammation levels at home. One, ask a doctor for a CRP, hs-CRP, or ESR blood test and get a clinical value. Two, order a mail-in finger-prick kit from Everlywell (about $99) or LetsGetChecked (about $89 to $119), or a Labcorp OnDemand test ($69) that you complete at a draw site, and receive a lab hs-CRP value in days. Three, use a kit or device you read yourself: Sensa turns a saliva sample into a 0 to 100 inflammation score in about 15 minutes ($49 a kit, less in multi-packs, no lab, no needle), and the COR One reads ESR from a drop of blood in about 30 minutes on a device plus monthly membership. Four, watch indirect signals such as resting heart rate, heart rate variability, sleep, and waist size. For a number a clinician can use, choose options one or two. For tracking your own trend as a wellness habit, choose option three. The two work well together.

Inflammation is the immune system's response to injury, infection, and strain, and in its low-grade, long-running form it tracks closely with sleep, diet, training load, stress, and body composition. That is why people want to measure it: not to diagnose anything, but to see what their habits are doing. Until recently the only way to get a number was a blood draw ordered by a doctor. Now there are four routes, and they answer different questions. This guide walks through each one, then shows you how to set up a routine that produces information you can act on.

Option 1: A Blood Test Ordered by Your Doctor

What you get. A clinical CRP, hs-CRP, or ESR value from a certified laboratory, interpreted by a clinician who knows your history. This is the reference standard, and for any medical question it is the only option on this list that counts.

What it costs and how long it takes. Often covered by insurance when ordered for a reason, otherwise anywhere from tens to a few hundred dollars depending on the panel. Results usually return within a day or two of the draw. The real cost is friction: an appointment, a needle, and a wait, which is why almost nobody does it monthly.

Option 2: A Mail-In or Lab-Visit Kit You Order Yourself

What you get. The same kind of lab hs-CRP value as option one, without the doctor's order. Everlywell ships a finger-prick kit (about $99, paired with vitamin D) with a prepaid return mailer. LetsGetChecked's blood kits run about $89 to $119 depending on the panel. Labcorp OnDemand sells an hs-CRP test for $69 that you complete with a standard draw at a Labcorp location. Prices were verified in August 2026 and are rechecked twice a month in our at-home CRP test comparison.

Time to result. Two to five business days after the lab receives your sample, plus shipping each way, so one to two weeks from ordering for mail-in kits. Labcorp posts results about a day after the draw.

Best for. A clinical number you can show a clinician, on your own schedule. Our guide to at-home hs-CRP kits with lab results compares these three in detail.

Option 3: A Kit or Device You Read at Home

Sensa, a saliva CRP test. You spit into the vial, wait for the color change, and scan the vial with the Sensa app. About 15 minutes after you start, the app shows a 0 to 100 inflammation score, sorts it into one of three tiers (In range, Worth watching, Time to reset), and adds it to your history so the trend is visible. No needle, no lab, no subscription. Kits are $49 for one, $99 for three, and $129 for four, with free shipping. Sensa is a general wellness product for tracking your own trend; it does not report an hs-CRP value and is not a diagnostic test. COR One, a blood-drop ESR device. COR Health sells a countertop device that reads erythrocyte sedimentation rate, a different inflammation marker, from a finger-prick drop of blood in about 30 minutes, with results sent to their app. It is sold as a device plus a monthly membership. If you want a blood-based reading and do not mind a lancet and a subscription, it is the other self-read option on the market. See our Sensa vs COR One comparison for a side-by-side.

Best for. Frequent testing. When a test takes 15 to 30 minutes and costs a fraction of a lab result, you will actually repeat it, and repetition is what turns a number into a trend.

Option 4: Indirect Signals From Wearables and Habits

What they tell you. Resting heart rate, heart rate variability, sleep quality, and waist circumference all move with inflammation, and most people already collect some of them. A rising resting heart rate and falling HRV during a stressful week, or a waist that creeps up over a year, are worth noticing.

What they cannot tell you. None of these is an inflammation measurement. They respond to many things at once, which is why they work best as context for a direct test rather than a replacement for one. Our wellness device comparison covers what wearables can and cannot show about inflammation.

How to Test Inflammation at Home With Sensa, Step by Step

1. Pick a consistent time. First thing in the morning, before food, coffee, or brushing your teeth, is the easiest to repeat. If mornings do not work, choose any time you can keep, and wait at least 30 minutes after eating, drinking anything but water, or brushing.

2. Check your mouth. Skip the test if your gums are bleeding or you have a mouth sore. Oral inflammation shows up in saliva and will push the reading up for reasons that have nothing to do with the rest of your body.

3. Run the test. Spit into the vial as the kit instructions show, close it, and wait for the color change. Set a timer so you read it at the same point every time.

4. Scan with the app. Place the vial on a plain white surface in good, even light with no glare, and let the app take the reading. Flash off. The app will warn you if fingers cover the liquid or the lighting is poor; follow the prompt and rescan.

5. Log the context. Note anything unusual: a cold, a hard workout the day before, a bad night's sleep, a vaccine, travel. Those notes explain most of the spikes you will see later.

6. Repeat on a schedule. Every one to two weeks is a good baseline rhythm. Add a test after a change you want to understand. The step-by-step how-to guide has photos of each step.

How to Read What You See

Trust the trend, not the point. Any single reading, lab or at-home, can be pushed around by a cold, a workout, or a poor night's sleep. Three or four readings spaced over a month tell you far more than one. Look for the direction of travel and for readings that stay in the same tier.

Expect spikes and let them pass. A short rise after illness, a vaccine, or an unusually hard training session is the system working as designed. Retest a week later before drawing any conclusion.

Act on habits, not on the number. The point of tracking is to see which changes help. Sleep, training load, alcohol, and ultra-processed food are the levers most people can move, and a trend that improves after a change is the feedback that makes the change stick. Common Mistakes

Testing at random times. Inconsistent timing adds noise that can swamp the signal you are looking for. Same time, same conditions, every time.

Reading one result as a verdict. One high reading is a reason to retest, not to worry. One low reading is not a clean bill of health.

Mixing up the scales. A Sensa score, an ESR, and a lab hs-CRP are three different measurements. Compare each one with its own history, not with each other.

Treating at-home testing as medical care. It is a wellness habit. Symptoms that worry you, or readings that stay high, are a reason to talk to a clinician, who can order a proper blood test and read it in context.

Testing inflammation at home is no longer a question of whether you can but of which route fits the question you are asking. A lab test gives you a clinical anchor. A self-read test gives you the rhythm between anchors. Use the one that matches your question, and keep the routine consistent enough that the trend has a chance to show itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the easiest way to test inflammation at home?

A self-read kit is the easiest because nothing leaves your house. With Sensa you spit into the vial, wait for the color change, and scan it with the app for a 0 to 100 score in about 15 minutes. Mail-in kits are also done at home but add a finger prick, a trip to the mailbox, and a wait of several business days for the lab.

Can you test CRP at home without a lab?

Yes. Sensa measures CRP in saliva and scores it on the phone, with no laboratory involved. The result is a wellness score for tracking your trend over time, not a clinical hs-CRP value in mg/L. If a doctor has asked for an hs-CRP number, a lab test is the right choice.

How often should you test inflammation at home?

For trend tracking, every one to two weeks at the same time of day is a practical rhythm, with an extra test after a change you want to understand, such as a new training block, a diet change, or a stressful stretch. Lab tests are usually once or twice a year unless a clinician suggests otherwise. Avoid reading too much into a single result in either case.

How much does an at-home inflammation test cost?

As of August 2026, lab-processed options run about $69 to $119 per result. Sensa is $49 for one kit, $99 for three, and $129 for four, about $32 per test in the four-pack, with free shipping. Device-based options such as the COR One combine a device with a monthly membership, so the cost depends on how long you keep testing.

When should you see a doctor instead of testing at home?

At-home testing is a wellness habit, not a substitute for medical care. See a clinician if you have symptoms that worry you, such as persistent fever, unexplained pain or swelling, or fatigue that does not lift, and whenever an at-home reading stays high across several tests. A clinician can order a proper blood test and interpret it in context.
