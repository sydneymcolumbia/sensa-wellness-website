Does Laughter Reduce Inflammation? What the Science Shows

Laughter does more than lighten a mood. Research in psychoneuroimmunology shows it dials down the stress hormones that keep inflammatory signaling elevated, boosts immune cell activity, and is associated with lower levels of circulating inflammatory markers. The catch is that the evidence base is smaller than for lifestyle heavyweights like sleep and exercise, and the effect sizes are modest.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes, in a meaningful if modest way. Genuine laughter reduces blood levels of cortisol and epinephrine, two stress hormones that are among the most direct drivers of inflammatory signaling in the body. Research from Loma Linda University found that watching a humor video increased natural killer cell activity and the output of the antiviral cytokine interferon-gamma, both indicators of a well-functioning immune state. A smaller clinical study in patients with rheumatoid arthritis found that a bout of hearty laughter was followed by a rapid drop in IL-6, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine. The laughter-specific evidence base is not large, but the broader literature on positive emotional states consistently links higher positive affect to lower C-reactive protein (CRP) and other inflammatory markers. Laughter appears to work primarily through the stress-hormone pathway, making it a genuinely useful, if underrated, part of an anti-inflammatory lifestyle.

When Norman Cousins famously checked himself out of a hospital in the 1970s and watched Marx Brothers films to help manage his painful inflammatory condition, he was dismissed by many in the medical establishment as eccentric. Decades of research in psychoneuroimmunology, the study of how psychological states interact with immune function, have given his intuition a credible scientific foundation. Laughter is no longer the sole province of self-help books. It sits at the intersection of stress biology, neuroendocrinology, and immunology, and the findings from that intersection are genuinely interesting.

Psychoneuroimmunology is the field of research that studies the bidirectional communication between the nervous system, the endocrine (hormone) system, and the immune system. It provides the scientific framework for understanding how mental and emotional states influence physical inflammation.

How Stress Hormones Connect Laughter to Inflammation

The most direct path from laughter to inflammation runs through the stress hormone system. Cortisol and epinephrine (adrenaline) are released by the adrenal glands during physical or psychological stress, and both have well-documented downstream effects on immune cell behavior. Acutely and briefly, they can suppress certain immune functions; chronically elevated, they paradoxically promote inflammation by upregulating pro-inflammatory signaling pathways including NF-kappa-B, the master switch that turns on the genes for cytokines like IL-6, IL-1-beta, and TNF-alpha. If laughter measurably lowers these hormones, it should, in theory, ease the inflammatory pressure they create.

Research published in 1989 provided direct evidence for this hormonal shift. In a controlled study at Loma Linda University, researchers measured serum levels of cortisol, epinephrine, dopamine metabolites, and several other neuroendocrine markers in healthy male subjects before, during, and after watching a one-hour humor video, compared with control subjects who did not watch it. The experimental group showed significantly greater decreases in cortisol and epinephrine than controls, along with lower levels of a dopamine metabolite. The researchers concluded that the "mirthful laughter experience appears to reduce serum levels of cortisol, dopac, epinephrine, and growth hormone," describing implications for reversing the classical neuroendocrine stress response (Berk et al., 1989). This foundational study made the mechanism plausible: laughter acts like a physiological counterweight to stress, pulling stress hormone levels toward a baseline that is less hospitable to chronic inflammation.

Natural Killer Cells and the Immune Surveillance Picture

A follow-up series of studies by the same Loma Linda group extended the picture from stress hormones to immune cell behavior. In a multivariate repeated-measures design involving 52 healthy men, subjects who viewed a humor video for one hour showed statistically significant increases in natural killer cell activity (a type of immune cell that identifies and destroys virus-infected and abnormal cells), immunoglobulins G, A, and M, and the cytokine interferon-gamma, a key antiviral and immunoregulatory signal. Several of these effects persisted for 12 hours after the humor experience ended. B cells, activated T cells, and helper T cells also increased. The researchers concluded that "modulation of neuroimmune parameters during and following the humor-associated eustress of laughter may provide beneficial health effects for wellness" (Berk et al., 2001).

This is an important finding to interpret carefully. Increases in natural killer cells and immunoglobulins do not directly mean lower CRP or fewer pro-inflammatory cytokines. They reflect an immune state that is actively engaged and well-regulated rather than the suppressed or dysregulated state associated with chronic high stress. The connection to inflammation specifically is indirect: a well-functioning immune system, supported by lower chronic stress hormone levels, is less likely to generate the sustained low-grade inflammatory output that drives elevated CRP. But the immune activation picture from the Berk studies is about immune competence, not a direct anti-inflammatory signal, and it is worth keeping that distinction in mind.

What Happens to Inflammatory Cytokines During Laughter

One of the more striking findings in the laughter literature involves IL-6 levels in patients with rheumatoid arthritis, a condition defined by chronic, high-grade inflammation. In a study examining the neuroendocrine-immune system in RA patients versus healthy controls, Japanese researchers measured multiple biomarkers at baseline and after various emotional experiences. They found that in RA patients, whose serum IL-6 levels were already significantly elevated above controls, "serum IL-6 levels in RA were significantly higher than in controls, and fell rapidly to as low as half of their initial value after a bout of hearty laughter." The authors suggested that "adequate mental intervention might serve to modulate the neuroendocrine-immune system" in this population (Yoshino and Mukai, 2003).

A reduction in IL-6 to roughly half of its elevated baseline is clinically meaningful in the context of RA, where IL-6 is a central driver of joint inflammation and systemic disease activity. However, this was a small observational study without a randomized design, and it involved a population with far higher baseline inflammation than a healthy adult. It would be an overreach to conclude that laughter delivers a dramatic IL-6 reduction in generally healthy people. What it does suggest is that the pathway through which laughter affects the immune-inflammatory system is real enough to produce measurable biomarker changes even in a condition as severe as active rheumatoid arthritis. For people carrying lower-grade inflammation, the effect would likely be smaller in absolute terms but still directionally relevant.

Positive Affect, CRP, and What the Broader Evidence Shows

The most rigorous and generalizable data connecting emotional states to inflammatory markers comes not from laughter-specific trials but from studies of positive affect, the broader category that includes contentment, joy, and calm well-being. This research base is larger, and it consistently finds that people who experience more positive emotional states carry lower inflammatory markers. A 2016 longitudinal study published in Psychosomatic Medicine measured high- and low-arousal positive affect and inflammatory markers including CRP, sTNF-RII, and interleukin-1 receptor antagonist in 186 breast cancer survivors over 12 months. Low-arousal positive affect, the kind captured by feelings such as calm, at ease, and relaxed, was significantly and independently associated with lower CRP, and this association held up through 6- and 12-month follow-ups after adjusting for confounders including fatigue (Moreno et al., 2016).

A 2026 systematic review in Brain, Behavior, and Immunity: Health reinforced these findings in the same population. Pooling studies that used validated positive affect measures and objective inflammatory biomarkers in women with breast cancer, the review found that "higher low-arousal positive affect was associated with lower C-reactive protein in longitudinal analyses" and that affiliative positive emotions were associated with enhanced immune signaling patterns. The authors concluded that "positive affect may be associated with reduced inflammatory activity or more adaptive immune functioning," while calling for larger studies with better standardization of affect measures and biomarker assessment (Frankowska et al., 2026).

Laughter fits naturally within the positive affect literature because it is one of the most reliably positive emotional experiences humans have. The calm, satisfied feeling after genuine laughter is precisely the low-arousal positive affect state that the CRP literature identifies as most protective. The stress hormone reductions documented in the Berk studies provide a plausible mechanism connecting that emotional state to the inflammatory marker reductions documented in the positive affect literature. Taken together, the two lines of evidence support a coherent story: laughter reduces cortisol and epinephrine, promotes a calmer and better-regulated immune state, and contributes to the low-arousal positive emotional tone associated with lower CRP over time.

What Laughter Cannot Do

Honesty about the limits of this research matters as much as enthusiasm for it. The laughter-specific evidence base is small. The most-cited studies come from one research group, used all-male samples, were not always fully blinded or randomized, and measured short-term effects rather than long-term inflammatory trends. No large randomized controlled trial has yet shown that a sustained laughter intervention, such as several weeks of regular humor engagement, produces clinically meaningful reductions in CRP or other markers in a generally healthy population. That evidence gap is significant, and it means claims about laughter "curing" any disease or being a primary treatment for any inflammatory condition have no scientific basis.

Laughter also cannot compensate for the major structural drivers of chronic inflammation. Excess body fat, particularly visceral fat, is one of the strongest determinants of elevated CRP and inflammatory cytokines. Poor sleep, a diet heavy in ultra-processed foods and added sugars, physical inactivity, and chronic unmanaged stress each contribute more to total inflammatory load than any benefit laughter can realistically provide. If the rest of the picture is poor, laughing more will not fix it. Laughter works at the margin, supporting an already reasonable foundation, rather than as a substitute for one.

How to Get the Real Thing

Not all laughter is created equal, and this distinction matters for the research. Forced or social laughter, the kind people produce to signal politeness or agreement, does not produce the same neuroendocrine changes as genuine, spontaneous, belly laughter. The studies that found measurable effects used humor that subjects found genuinely funny, not laughter on demand. This is actually good news in practical terms: the prescription is to pursue things that genuinely make you laugh, not to perform positivity. Comedy films, stand-up specials, funny friends, absurd situations, and moments of shared humor in relationships all qualify. The quality of the emotion matters, not the social display of it.

Social laughter and connection are intertwined in important ways. Most genuine laughter happens in social contexts, and the social connection that surrounds it carries its own anti-inflammatory benefit. Research consistently shows that loneliness and social isolation are associated with higher CRP and worse immune function, while strong social ties are associated with lower inflammatory markers. Laughter shared with others therefore carries a dual benefit: the direct neuroendocrine and immune effects of the laughter itself, and the social bonding that laughter both signals and strengthens. This is one reason the effect of humor on health is difficult to separate cleanly from the effect of social connection, and why treating them as a package is more practically useful than trying to isolate either one.

Laughter as One Piece of a Larger Picture

The science on laughter and inflammation is not a reason to replace evidence-based interventions with comedy, but it is a legitimate reason to stop treating amusement as frivolous. The pathway is real: genuine laughter lowers stress hormones that feed inflammatory signaling, supports a well-regulated immune state, and fits within the broader pattern of positive affect being associated with lower CRP. The clinical significance of any single bout of laughter is modest, but the cumulative effect of a life with more genuine positive emotion is larger, and it operates alongside exercise, sleep quality, dietary patterns, and stress management rather than in competition with them.

Tracking your CRP over time can help you see whether the lifestyle picture you are building is reflected in your inflammatory markers. Because CRP integrates inputs from sleep, diet, stress, body composition, physical activity, and even your emotional state, it functions as a running summary of how your biology is responding to the life you are living. If you are working on stress management, adding meaningful social time, sleeping better, and eating well, watching those changes show up in your numbers is motivating and informative. Persistent elevations in CRP are always worth discussing with a healthcare provider, since they can signal a range of conditions that warrant evaluation. But for most people, CRP is a general wellness signal, and a downward trend over months is exactly the kind of feedback that keeps the effort worthwhile.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does laughter reduce inflammation?

Yes, in a modest but real way. Genuine laughter lowers cortisol and epinephrine, two stress hormones that are direct drivers of inflammatory signaling. Research shows laughter also increases natural killer cell activity and interferon-gamma output, and one study found that hearty laughter was followed by a rapid drop in IL-6 in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. The broader literature on positive emotional states consistently links higher positive affect to lower CRP over time.

How does laughter affect inflammatory markers like CRP?

Laughter does not appear to lower CRP acutely in a single session in the way that, say, an anti-inflammatory drug does. Rather, it works through the stress-hormone pathway: by reducing cortisol and epinephrine, laughter dampens one of the main inputs that keeps NF-kappa-B signaling and pro-inflammatory cytokine production elevated. Over time, a life with more genuine positive emotion and lower chronic stress hormone activity is associated with lower circulating CRP, as studies linking positive affect to inflammatory markers consistently show.

Is there a difference between genuine and forced laughter for inflammation?

Yes. The neuroendocrine and immune changes documented in research used genuine, spontaneous laughter in response to humor that subjects actually found funny, not laughter on command. Forced laughter does not produce the same reductions in cortisol or epinephrine, and it would not be expected to carry the same inflammatory benefits. The practical implication is that the goal is to seek out things, situations, and people that provoke real amusement rather than trying to perform positivity.

Can laughter replace other anti-inflammatory lifestyle practices?

No. Laughter operates at the margin of a larger inflammatory picture. The biggest drivers of chronic low-grade inflammation are excess body fat, poor sleep, a diet high in ultra-processed foods and added sugar, physical inactivity, and chronic stress. Laughter reduces stress hormone output and supports immune regulation, but it cannot compensate for major structural lifestyle problems. It is best understood as a useful and enjoyable complement to an anti-inflammatory lifestyle, not a substitute for its foundations.
