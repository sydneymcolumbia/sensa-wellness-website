Can a Daily Oral Hyaluronic Acid Supplement Lower Inflammation? What a New Trial Found

Hyaluronic acid is best known as a skincare ingredient and joint injection. A newly published randomized trial now asks whether taking it orally every day can produce measurable changes in inflammation markers circulating in the blood, and the results are more interesting than most people expected.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in Scientific Reports in November 2026 found that healthy adults taking 120 mg of oral hyaluronic acid daily for 12 weeks showed significantly lower levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1 beta) compared with placebo. The result is intriguing and biologically plausible, but the trial was partly funded by a hyaluronic acid manufacturer, the sample was relatively small, and the authors call for further work. This is a promising early signal, not a settled conclusion.

Hyaluronic acid gets most of its press from two very different places: the skincare aisle, where it appears in serums and moisturizers, and the orthopedist's office, where it has been injected into arthritic joints for decades. The idea that swallowing a hyaluronic acid capsule every day could do something measurable to inflammation inside the body has been around for years, but the evidence was thin and mixed. A new trial published in late November 2026 adds an important and carefully designed data point to that debate, and the finding is specific enough to be worth understanding in detail.

Hyaluronic acid (also called hyaluronan or HA) is a glycosaminoglycan, a long chain of repeating sugar units, that forms a core part of the extracellular matrix in connective tissue, joints, skin, and the vitreous fluid of the eye. It is produced naturally by the body and broken down continuously by enzymes and reactive oxygen species.

The New Study: What Researchers Did

The trial was published November 21, 2026 in Scientific Reports under the title "Systemic immune and metabolomic responses to oral hyaluronic acid in a randomized placebo-controlled clinical study." It was conducted by a team including researchers from Masaryk University in the Czech Republic alongside scientists employed at Contipro, a Czech biotech company that develops and manufactures hyaluronic acid products. That funding relationship is an important detail to keep in mind when interpreting the results, and the authors disclose it clearly.

The design was a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial lasting 12 weeks. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three groups: 60 mg of oral hyaluronic acid (SH60), 120 mg of oral hyaluronic acid (SH120), or a matched placebo taken daily. The analysis went beyond the usual panel of blood markers. Using untargeted plasma proteomics and metabolomics, the team measured a broad sweep of immune proteins and metabolic compounds in participants' blood rather than testing a predetermined short list. This kind of omics approach can reveal effects that a narrow cytokine panel would miss, though it also generates more statistical comparisons and requires careful interpretation.

The key finding for inflammation was dose-dependent and concentrated in the higher-dose group. Participants taking 120 mg per day showed significantly lower plasma levels of CRP and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1 beta) compared with the placebo group. The researchers also found that levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA) fell in the same group. This secondary finding is counterintuitive at first glance, but the authors interpret it as a sign that there was less need for counter-regulatory anti-inflammatory signaling because the pro-inflammatory load had itself decreased. Arachidonic acid, a fatty acid that serves as a precursor to pro-inflammatory eicosanoids, was also reduced in the 120 mg group. The 60 mg group did not show the same pattern, suggesting that if the effect is real, it requires a threshold dose to become detectable.

The study team describes the overall shift as a modest movement toward a less inflammatory and more metabolically favorable systemic profile. Metabolomic markers including fetuin-A, adipsin, and several fatty acid metabolites also changed in the 120 mg group, which the researchers read as consistent with reduced low-grade systemic inflammation rather than a dramatic suppression of the immune system. That framing is accurate: none of these changes suggest immune compromise, and the participants remained healthy throughout the trial.

You can read the full open-access paper at nature.com/articles/s41598-026-80937-9.

Why Oral Hyaluronic Acid Might Do Anything at All in the Blood

The obvious question is how swallowing a large sugar polymer ends up changing inflammation markers circulating in the blood. Hyaluronic acid as a whole molecule is very large. For years, the assumption was that it could not survive digestion, that enzymes in the gut would break it into fragments too small to do anything useful, and that those fragments would never reach systemic circulation in meaningful amounts. The reality appears to be more complicated than that early dismissal.

Research over the past two decades has established that hyaluronic acid is not fully degraded to monosaccharides in the gut. Digestion does fragment it into oligosaccharides, short chains of repeating units, and evidence from animal and human studies suggests these fragments can cross the intestinal epithelium and enter the bloodstream. Once in circulation, they interact with cell surface receptors including CD44, RHAMM, and toll-like receptors 2 and 4 (TLR2 and TLR4). How those interactions affect inflammation is where the size of the fragments becomes critical.

The molecular weight of hyaluronic acid determines whether it tends to suppress or promote inflammation, a distinction well-established in the basic science literature. As reviewed in research on hyaluronan-mediated regulation of inflammation, the native high-molecular-weight form found in healthy connective tissue interacts with CD44 in ways that restrain immune activation and prevent excessive inflammatory responses. When tissues are damaged or stressed, enzymes and reactive oxygen species fragment this large polymer into low-molecular-weight pieces, and those fragments act as danger signals, activating TLR4 and CD44 to recruit immune cells and amplify inflammatory cytokine production (Krasinski and Tchorzewski, 2007).

The supplement used in the Czech trial contains a full-spectrum preparation of hyaluronic acid, meaning it includes both higher and lower molecular weight chains. Exactly how the fragments produced from digesting this preparation interact with gut-associated lymphoid tissue, and whether systemic immune effects depend on absorption of intact oligosaccharides or on some indirect gut-based signaling mechanism, is not yet clear. The authors themselves note that microbiome analysis and more targeted mechanistic studies are needed to determine what is happening biochemically. The metabolomic data provides a detailed picture of the endpoint, but the pathway from capsule to lower CRP remains incompletely mapped.

The Molecular Weight Story: A Key Nuance

The dual behavior of hyaluronic acid, anti-inflammatory at high molecular weight and pro-inflammatory at low molecular weight, is one of the more elegant examples of how the same molecule can play opposite biological roles depending on its physical form. In healthy tissue, the extracellular matrix is dominated by high-molecular-weight hyaluronan chains, often millions of Daltons long, that form hydrated gels, provide structural support, and suppress excessive immune signaling by occupying CD44 receptors without triggering TLR-mediated danger responses.

Inflammation and tissue damage break those long chains into fragments, and those fragments become pro-inflammatory signals in their own right. Research examining low-molecular-weight HA in the lungs found that these fragments activate neutrophils, macrophages, and dendritic cells, amplifying innate immune responses in injured tissue (Hoarau et al., 2022). A study of HA oligosaccharides in thyroid cells showed that short fragments engage TLR2, TLR4, and CD44 to activate NF-kappa-B, the master transcription factor for inflammatory gene expression, increasing production of IL-1 beta and IL-6 (D'Ascola et al., 2020). In this context, low-molecular-weight hyaluronan fragments essentially mimic the danger signals that tissues generate after injury.

This complexity matters for interpreting the new oral supplement trial. If the fragments reaching systemic circulation from a digested HA supplement were predominantly of the pro-inflammatory low-molecular-weight variety, the expectation would be a rise in CRP and cytokines, not a fall. The Czech trial's finding that CRP fell rather than rose suggests that either the circulating fragments are predominantly of a molecular weight that engages CD44 in an anti-inflammatory mode, or that gut-based signaling effects are driving systemic changes that do not depend on fragment size in circulation. Both hypotheses are consistent with the available mechanistic literature, and neither is yet proven for oral supplement use in humans.

What the Limitations Mean for Interpretation

Three limitations of the Czech trial stand out as genuinely important for anyone deciding how much weight to give these results.

First, the funding relationship. Several of the authors are employees of Contipro, the company that makes the SH120 and SH60 preparations used in the trial. Industry-funded trials are not automatically flawed, and the design elements of this study, randomization, blinding, placebo control, and objective omics measurements, are genuinely rigorous. But the history of nutrition supplement research shows that industry-sponsored studies tend to find more favorable results than independent replications. The honest interpretation is that this trial provides a credible positive signal that now needs independent confirmation.

Second, the sample size and lifestyle controls. The trial enrolled 150 healthy adults, which is respectable for this type of nutrition research but not large by clinical trial standards. The authors acknowledge the absence of strict controls over participants' diet and physical activity during the trial period, which means that if, for example, participants in the 120 mg group happened to eat less red meat or exercise more during the study, that could partially account for the CRP differences.

Third, the population and generalizability. This trial was conducted in healthy adults, not people with elevated CRP or diagnosed inflammatory conditions. The baseline CRP levels of participants are not explicitly stated in the reporting available so far. If participants already had low CRP at the start, the clinical meaning of a further reduction is different from, say, lowering CRP in someone with chronic low-grade inflammation driven by metabolic disease. The authors themselves emphasize that further studies are needed before clinical recommendations could be drawn.

How This Fits the Broader Hyaluronic Acid Literature

Earlier clinical research on oral hyaluronic acid was focused almost entirely on joints and skin, and the inflammatory story was largely secondary. Multiple randomized trials reported that oral hyaluronic acid preparations improved knee pain and function in people with osteoarthritis, with some trials also showing reductions in joint-specific inflammatory markers. Skin hydration and wrinkle depth improvement were reported in separate trials, most notably in healthy adults taking relatively low doses. CRP was not the primary outcome in any of these earlier studies, and the results for systemic inflammatory markers were generally inconclusive.

The 2026 Czech trial is notable precisely because it used systemic inflammation as a primary outcome and deployed omics tools broad enough to capture changes that targeted cytokine panels would miss. Finding a CRP reduction in healthy adults without an obvious disease burden, using an untargeted approach that pre-specified no favorite cytokines, is a methodologically stronger design than asking "does this supplement lower IL-6 specifically?" and then reporting only that result. The breadth of the metabolomic analysis also provides a richer story: the changes in arachidonic acid and other lipid metabolites suggest a shift in the whole inflammatory signaling environment, not just suppression of one downstream marker.

At the same time, an earlier small positive signal in one industry-funded trial should not be read as proof that everyone taking oral HA will see CRP improvements. The story of omega-3 fatty acids, whose convincing anti-inflammatory effects in short trials did not reliably translate into reduced cardiovascular events in large long-term trials, is a useful reminder that moving a blood marker is not the same as changing health outcomes. CRP is a valuable wellness signal to track, and a reduction is generally favorable, but no supplement finding from a 12-week trial warrants changing your health strategy before independent replication.

What It Means for Tracking Inflammation

If you are already tracking your CRP as a general wellness signal, the Czech trial gives you something specific to watch for if you happen to take oral hyaluronic acid for joint comfort or skin benefits. The trial design means you would need at least 12 weeks on a consistent daily dose, with a comparison point from before you started, to see whether a change in your numbers tracks the supplement. That is exactly the kind of observation that personal CRP tracking makes possible, though any pattern you notice is an individual data point and not a clinical diagnosis of anything.

The broader message from this research is that inflammation is a system with multiple inputs, and the supplement industry regularly identifies compounds that move markers in short trials without fully explaining the biology or demonstrating lasting benefit. Hyaluronic acid is a genuinely interesting case because the mechanistic story is plausible, the molecule is endogenous, and the safety profile from decades of topical and intravenous use is reassuring. Those factors make it a more credible candidate than many. But credible and proven are different things, and the right response to this trial is curiosity rather than certainty. The finding is worth watching as replication studies appear, and it is worth discussing with a healthcare provider if you are considering adding any new supplement to your routine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does oral hyaluronic acid reduce inflammation?

A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in Scientific Reports in November 2026 found that 120 mg of oral hyaluronic acid taken daily for 12 weeks significantly reduced CRP and IL-1 beta in healthy adults compared with placebo. The result is biologically plausible and methodologically credible, but the trial was partly funded by a hyaluronic acid manufacturer and independent replication is needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.

How might oral hyaluronic acid affect CRP?

Digestion fragments oral hyaluronic acid into oligosaccharides that can cross the intestinal lining and enter the bloodstream. High-molecular-weight hyaluronan is known to suppress inflammatory signaling by occupying CD44 receptors without triggering TLR-based danger responses. If the fragments reaching systemic circulation retain a molecular weight profile that engages this anti-inflammatory pathway, a reduction in CRP is mechanistically plausible. The Czech 2026 trial also found reduced arachidonic acid, a precursor to pro-inflammatory eicosanoids, which supports this interpretation.

What dose of oral hyaluronic acid was tested?

The 2026 Czech trial tested two doses: 60 mg and 120 mg per day for 12 weeks. Only the 120 mg group showed significant reductions in CRP and IL-1 beta compared with placebo. The 60 mg group did not show the same pattern, suggesting a threshold dose may be needed for detectable systemic effects.

Is oral hyaluronic acid safe?

Hyaluronic acid has been used topically and by intravenous infusion in clinical settings for decades with a reassuring safety profile. Oral hyaluronic acid supplements are generally considered safe in the doses studied in clinical trials. The 2026 Czech trial, the gingivitis trials, and earlier skin and joint studies have not reported serious adverse events from oral use. If you are considering any new supplement, the appropriate step is to discuss it with a healthcare provider, especially if you take other medications or have an existing health condition.
