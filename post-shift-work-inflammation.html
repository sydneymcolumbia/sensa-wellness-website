Does Shift Work Cause Inflammation?

Working nights and rotating schedules forces your body to run against its internal clock. A growing body of research shows that this misalignment can nudge inflammatory markers like CRP upward, and it may help explain why shift work is linked to heart disease.

Written by Sydney Murphy, CMO & Digital Products Officer. Reviewed by the Sensa Wellness editorial team. Written to reflect current, publicly available inflammation research.

The short answer

Yes, the evidence suggests shift work can raise inflammation. When your work schedule is out of sync with your internal body clock, a state researchers call circadian misalignment, controlled laboratory studies show measurable increases in inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6, and TNF-alpha. The effect is not huge from any single night, but repeated over months and years it may contribute to the higher rates of heart disease and metabolic problems seen in shift workers. Good sleep habits, meal timing, and light management can help blunt the impact.

Roughly one in five workers in developed economies works some form of non-daytime schedule: overnight nursing shifts, rotating factory rotas, early-morning transport routes, and the long, irregular hours of first responders and healthcare trainees. For decades, epidemiologists have noticed that these workers carry a higher burden of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and obesity than their day-working peers, even after accounting for smoking, diet, and body weight. Something about the schedule itself appears to matter.

Inflammation has emerged as one of the most compelling explanations for that "something." Chronic, low-grade inflammation is a shared driver of the same conditions that shift workers develop more often, and there is now direct experimental evidence that misaligning the body clock raises inflammatory signaling independently of how much sleep a person loses. Understanding this connection helps shift workers make sense of their risk, and it points to practical steps that can reduce it.

Circadian misalignment is the mismatch between your internal circadian clock and your behavioral cycle of sleeping, waking, and eating. Night and rotating shift work is the most common cause, forcing the body to be active and digesting food during its biological night.

What the Research Shows About Shift Work and CRP

The strongest evidence comes from tightly controlled laboratory studies that separate the effect of the body clock from the effect of sleep loss. In a landmark experiment led by researchers at Brigham and Women's Hospital and Harvard Medical School in Boston, healthy adults underwent simulated circadian misalignment, a rapid inversion of their sleep and eating schedule of the kind shift workers experience. Misalignment increased 24-hour levels of interleukin-6, C-reactive protein, resistin, and TNF-alpha by 3 to 29 percent, while also raising blood pressure, all while the total amount of sleep was held constant (Morris et al., PNAS, 2016). Because the sleep opportunity was identical in both conditions, the inflammatory rise could be attributed to the clock mismatch itself.

A follow-up study in experienced, chronic shift workers reinforced the point. Using a randomized crossover design that compared simulated night work against simulated day work, the researchers found that circadian misalignment increased 24-hour high-sensitivity CRP by 11 percent (Morris et al., Journal of Biological Rhythms, 2017). This mattered because it showed the effect was not limited to people unaccustomed to night work; even bodies with years of shift experience still mounted an inflammatory response when pushed against their clock.

Population studies point in the same direction, though with more noise. Real-world data are messier than laboratory data because shift workers differ from day workers in diet, stress, and socioeconomic factors. Still, a study of female manufacturing workers in Korea found that, after adjusting for confounders, shift workers had significantly higher average high-sensitivity CRP than daytime workers (Kwak et al., Annals of Occupational and Environmental Medicine, 2019). And in a very large analysis of more than 350,000 UK Biobank participants, permanent night shift work and poor sleep quality were positively associated with higher blood pressure and inflammatory status (Kanki et al., Nature Communications, 2023).

Why Night Work Inflames the Body: The Circadian Mechanism

Nearly every cell in the body carries a molecular clock, and these clocks are synchronized by a master timekeeper in the brain that takes its main cue from light. This system does more than tell you when to feel sleepy. It gates the immune response, tuning inflammatory genes up and down across the 24-hour day so that immune activity peaks when it is most useful and quiets down at other times.

The clock directly restrains inflammation during your biological rest phase. Core clock proteins normally suppress NF-kB, the master switch for inflammatory gene expression, during specific windows of the day. When you stay awake, active, and eating through your biological night, that timed suppression is disrupted. Inflammatory signaling that should be dialed down stays elevated, and the daily rhythm of cortisol, a hormone with powerful anti-inflammatory effects, is flattened and mistimed. For a deeper look at this machinery, see our guide to circadian rhythm and inflammation.

Eating at the wrong biological time compounds the problem. Shift workers often eat their largest meals during the night, when the body is metabolically primed for rest rather than digestion. This mistimed eating impairs glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, promotes the accumulation of inflammatory visceral fat, and adds another layer of inflammatory pressure on top of the clock disruption itself. Notably, some research suggests that restricting eating to daytime hours during night shifts can blunt these cardiovascular and metabolic changes, hinting that meal timing is a modifiable lever.

Which Shift Patterns Carry the Most Risk

Not all shift work is equal, and the details of a schedule shape how much inflammatory strain it imposes.

Rotating shifts may be especially disruptive. Schedules that flip between day and night, or rotate quickly, give the body clock no stable target to lock onto. Just as the clock begins to adapt, the schedule changes again, producing a state of chronic realignment stress. Permanent night workers, by contrast, sometimes achieve partial adaptation, although true full adaptation is rare because exposure to daylight on days off and during the commute keeps pulling the clock back toward a day schedule.

Long hours amplify the effect. A study of Korean workers found that roughly 25 percent had elevated high-sensitivity CRP, and it identified a significant interaction between long working hours and shift work in raising CRP, particularly in middle-aged women (Lee et al., Scandinavian Journal of Work, Environment & Health, 2021). In other words, the combination of a non-standard schedule and extended hours appears to compound inflammatory risk beyond what either does alone.

Cumulative years of exposure matter. The inflammatory response to a single misaligned night is modest. What concerns researchers is the accumulation over a career. The same low-grade elevation, repeated thousands of times across years of night work, is thought to contribute to the arterial stiffening and metabolic changes that eventually translate into disease risk.

Shift Work, Inflammation, and Long-Term Disease Risk

The reason inflammation matters here is that it is not just a passive marker; it is an active participant in the diseases that shift workers develop more often. Chronic low-grade inflammation drives the plaque formation at the heart of cardiovascular disease, contributes to the insulin resistance behind type 2 diabetes, and is woven into the metabolic dysfunction that accompanies weight gain.

Inflammation may also help explain the more immediate symptoms shift workers report. In a seven-year prospective study, shift work combined with elevated CRP was associated with a higher risk of chronic pain, with the combination carrying an odds ratio of about 1.76 for any chronic pain, a link that CRP alone did not fully explain (Christensen et al., International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health, 2021). This suggests inflammation could be one of the pathways connecting disrupted schedules to the aches, fatigue, and reduced resilience that many shift workers describe.

It is worth keeping perspective. Shift work is a risk factor, not a sentence, and many shift workers stay healthy for decades. The inflammatory changes seen in studies are real but modest, and they sit alongside other modifiable factors. The practical takeaway is not alarm but attention: if your schedule is working against your biology, it is worth actively managing the factors you can control.

How Shift Workers Can Lower Inflammation

The good news is that most of the levers that reduce circadian and inflammatory strain are within reach, even on a demanding schedule.

  1. Protect your daytime sleep. Sleep is when the body clears inflammatory debris and rebalances the immune system. Make daytime sleep as dark, cool, and quiet as possible with blackout curtains, an eye mask, and earplugs or white noise. Even partial protection of sleep quantity and quality reduces the inflammatory penalty. Our guide to sleep and inflammation covers the mechanisms in detail.
  2. Time your light exposure deliberately. Bright light anchors the clock. During night shifts, some exposure to bright light can support alertness, but wearing sunglasses on the commute home and avoiding bright morning light before daytime sleep helps the clock settle. On days off, getting morning daylight can help stabilize your rhythm.
  3. Shift your eating toward daytime hours. Where possible, eat your main meals before or after your shift rather than in the middle of the biological night, and keep overnight food lighter. This aligns digestion closer to when your metabolism is primed for it and may reduce the metabolic and inflammatory cost of night work.
  4. Keep moving. Regular exercise is one of the most reliable anti-inflammatory interventions available and also improves sleep quality. Even short, consistent activity helps counteract the metabolic drift that shift work encourages.
  5. Favor an anti-inflammatory diet. A pattern rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, fish, and olive oil, and lower in ultra-processed food and added sugar, supports a lower inflammatory baseline. This is a foundational lever regardless of when you eat.
  6. Track your inflammation over time. Because the effects of shift work accumulate slowly, watching your inflammatory trend is more useful than any single reading. Objective data tells you whether the habits you are building are actually bending the curve.

Tracking Your Inflammation If You Work Shifts

Shift work is exactly the kind of slow, cumulative stressor that benefits from long-term monitoring rather than one-off testing. A single CRP reading tells you little on its own, especially since a recent hard workout, a minor cold, or a poor stretch of sleep can temporarily nudge it up. What is informative is the direction of your CRP over months and years, and whether the changes you make to sleep, light, meal timing, and movement are holding that trend low.

C-reactive protein is the most practical marker for this because it reflects the same IL-6-driven inflammatory signaling that circadian misalignment appears to activate. Sensa is designed to make checking CRP simple, letting you measure it at home without a needle or a clinic visit, so a reading becomes a routine data point rather than a rare event. For a shift worker, that means you can see whether a new sleep setup or eating schedule is measurably reducing your inflammatory load, and catch an upward drift early while lifestyle changes can still address it. CRP is a general wellness marker rather than a diagnosis, so persistent elevations are worth discussing with a healthcare provider, particularly given the cardiovascular considerations that come with long-term shift work.

Shift work asks your body to run against one of its most fundamental rhythms. You may not be able to change your schedule, but understanding how it affects inflammation gives you a clear set of levers to pull, and a simple way to check whether they are working.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does working night shifts raise CRP?

Controlled laboratory studies show that circadian misalignment, the kind produced by night and rotating shift work, raises high-sensitivity CRP even when total sleep is held constant. One study in chronic shift workers found an 11 percent increase in 24-hour hs-CRP during simulated night work compared with day work. Any single night has a modest effect, but repeated exposure over years is what concerns researchers.

Why does shift work cause inflammation?

Your body has an internal clock that normally restrains inflammatory signaling during your biological rest phase. Being awake, active, and eating during the biological night disrupts that timing, so inflammatory genes that should be dialed down stay elevated. Mistimed eating, poor daytime sleep, and altered blood pressure and stress hormones add further inflammatory pressure.

Which shift schedules are worst for inflammation?

Rapidly rotating shifts tend to be especially disruptive because the body clock never gets a stable schedule to adapt to. Long working hours combined with shift work appear to compound the effect, and cumulative years of night work matter more than any single shift. Permanent night workers may achieve partial adaptation, though rarely full adaptation.

Can shift workers reduce inflammation?

Yes. Protecting daytime sleep with a dark, cool, quiet room, managing light exposure, shifting most eating toward daytime hours, exercising regularly, and following an anti-inflammatory diet all help reduce the inflammatory strain of shift work. Tracking CRP over time lets you see whether these habits are actually lowering your inflammatory load.
